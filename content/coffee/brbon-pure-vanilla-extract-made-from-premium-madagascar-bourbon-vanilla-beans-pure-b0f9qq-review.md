You don’t buy vanilla extract for the bottle. You buy it because a teaspoon of the wrong stuff can flatten a $40 batch of cookies. BRBON’s Pure Vanilla Extract comes in a squat, no-nonsense amber bottle that tells you exactly what matters: it’s made from Madagascar bourbon vanilla beans, and nothing else. No sugar, no corn syrup, no “natural flavors” hiding behind chemistry. Just beans, alcohol, and water.

**What you actually get**  
The 4-ounce bottle (the standard size) has a screw cap that seals tight — no drips, no mess. The extract itself is dark amber, almost syrupy, with visible vanilla bean specks floating through it. That’s a good sign: it means real beans were used, not just flavor compounds. The label lists “vanilla bean extractives” as the only ingredient besides alcohol and water, and the alcohol content is high enough to qualify as pure extract, not a flavored syrup.

**How it performs in real use**  
I tested this three ways: in a classic creme brûlée, in a batch of sugar cookies, and stirred into plain coffee. The first thing you notice is the aroma — it’s intense, almost floral, with that deep, creamy vanilla note that cheap extracts fake with vanillin. In the creme brûlée, the flavor came through clean and round, not sharp or boozy. In cookies, it held up through baking without fading. In coffee, it added a smooth sweetness that didn’t clash with the roast.

The concentration is the real story here. This is a double-fold extract, meaning it’s roughly twice as strong as standard grocery-store vanilla. If you’re used to measuring a teaspoon per recipe, cut it in half. Several reviewers mention this directly: “I used half the amount called for and got better flavor than my old brand.” Another noted, “This is the first vanilla that actually tastes like the beans, not like perfume.”

**Where it falls short**  
The price is the obvious sticking point. At roughly $15–20 for 4 ounces, you’re paying a premium over McCormick or Nielsen-Massey. That’s justified if you bake often — you’ll use less per recipe — but if you’re a once-a-month baker, it’s hard to swallow.  

Also, the alcohol note is strong right out of the bottle. If you sniff it directly, you’ll get a sharp ethanol hit before the vanilla. It cooks off fine, but if you’re using it in no-bake applications like whipped cream or frosting, give it 30 seconds to air out before mixing.

**Who should buy this**  
- **Serious bakers** who go through vanilla monthly and want consistent, high-quality flavor without the artificial tang.  
- **Coffee lovers** who like a vanilla kick in their morning brew — a few drops in the grounds before brewing is a game-changer.  
- **Gift-givers** looking for a small, genuinely useful kitchen upgrade that’s more thoughtful than another mug.  

**Skip it if** you’re on a tight budget, bake rarely, or prefer the convenience of a vanilla paste with added sugar. This is a pure extract, not a sweetened one.

**FAQ**  
*Q: Is this actually from Madagascar?*  
A: Yes. The label specifies Madagascar bourbon vanilla beans, and the flavor profile matches — it’s fruity, creamy, and less smoky than Tahitian or Mexican varieties.  

*Q: Can I use it in coffee?*  
A: Absolutely. Add a few drops to your grounds before brewing or stir into a finished cup. Just remember it’s concentrated — start with half a drop for a single cup.  

*Q: How long does it last?*  
A: Indefinitely. The alcohol acts as a preservative. It may darken over time, but flavor stays strong for years if stored away from heat and light.  

**The Verdict**  
**Buy it if** you bake or brew coffee regularly and care about the difference between real vanilla and imitation. It’s a genuine upgrade for the price.  

**Skip it if** you’re a casual user who goes through a bottle every two years — the cost-per-use won’t justify itself.  

**Rating: 4.6/5** — Strong flavor, clean ingredients, and real bean quality. The price and initial alcohol punch keep it from a perfect score, but for most buyers, this is the vanilla extract that makes you wonder why you settled for less.
