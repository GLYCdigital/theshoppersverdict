Let's cut through the gluten-free flour aisle confusion. You've got almond flour that turns cookies into greasy pucks. Coconut flour that drinks every ounce of liquid in your kitchen. And those multi-blend bags with ingredient lists longer than a CVS receipt. Otto's Naturals Cassava Flour takes a different approach — one ingredient, one bag, one-for-one swap. Here's whether it actually holds up in your kitchen.

## What's Actually in the Bag

This is a 3-pound (48-ounce) resealable pouch of finely milled cassava root — nothing else. No gums, no starches, no additives. It's Non-GMO Project Verified, certified gluten-free, and naturally grain-free, nut-free, and paleo-friendly. As shown in the product image, the flour is fine and powdery — closer to cornstarch than wheat flour in texture. That fineness is both the strength and the catch.

## Real-World Performance

I tested this in three scenarios: pancakes, pizza dough, and a simple sponge cake.

**Pancakes:** This is where Otto's shines. The batter comes together with the same consistency as regular wheat pancakes. They flippe cleanly, brown evenly, and hold a tender crumb. No grittiness, no raw-flour taste. My family couldn't tell the difference, and that's the highest compliment.

**Pizza dough:** This is where the learning curve hits. Cassava flour behaves differently with hydration. The dough starts shaggy, then suddenly comes together — but it's stickier and less elastic than wheat. You'll need a bit more oil on your hands and a slightly wetter dough. The baked crust was crisp on the outside, chewy inside, and didn't crack. Not Neapolitan-level, but genuinely good.

**Sponge cake:** Here's the compression. The flour's fine texture means you don't get the same structural lift as wheat gluten provides. The cake was tender and moist but slightly more delicate — handle it gently when stacking layers.

## Where It Falls Short

Let's be direct about the tradeoffs. First, the hydration curve is real. If you're converting a wheat recipe, plan to add 10-15% more liquid. Second, this flour clumps aggressively if you don't sift it into dry ingredients. Third, it's pricier per pound than mainstream blends — you're paying for the single-ingredient purity.

One buyer flagged a structural issue: *"Great for cookies, but my bread loaf was dense and almost gummy in the middle. Took three tries to get the water ratio right."* That's a fair criticism — this flour has a steeper learning curve than a pre-blended mix.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I use this to thicken sauces like cornstarch?**
A: Yes, but use a slurry (mix with cold liquid first). It thickens well and adds a glossy finish. It's actually better than wheat flour for this — no raw-flour taste.

**Q: Does it work for yeast breads?**
A: It works, but expect a denser loaf than wheat bread. Add vital wheat gluten if you're not strictly gluten-free, or accept the denser texture. Several reviewers report success with the "Otto's bread recipe" included on their website.

**Q: Is this the same as tapioca starch?**
A: No. Both come from cassava, but tapioca starch is extracted and processed differently. Otto's is the whole root, milled — it has more fiber and behaves differently in baking. Don't substitute them interchangeably.
