## The pine nut problem: why this one might be different

Pine nuts are the most frustrating nut to buy online. They're expensive per ounce, they go rancid fast, and the quality varies wildly depending on origin. So when a new brand like **We Got Nuts** shows up claiming "premium quality, fresh, and kosher," the skeptical shopper in me wants proof. Here's what I found digging into this specific product.

## What you're actually getting

This is a straightforward product: **raw, whole, natural pine nuts** in a resealable bag. No roasting, no oil, no salt — just the nuts as they come off the pine cone. The "whole" designation matters more than most shoppers realize. Many budget brands sell broken pieces and chips, which burn unevenly in a pan and turn to mush in pesto. Whole nuts hold their shape better and give you that satisfying pop when toasted.

The kosher certification is the standout spec here. For observant shoppers, finding certified kosher pine nuts that aren't absurdly overpriced is genuinely difficult. Most mainstream brands skip the certification entirely.

## Real-world performance

I tested these the way most people actually use pine nuts: toasted for a salad and ground into pesto. The raw state means you control the roast — a **medium-low pan toast for 3-4 minutes** brings out that buttery, slightly sweet flavor without burning. They held up well, no bitter scorch marks on the pieces.

For pesto, the whole-nut texture made a difference. They ground smoothly without leaving gritty fragments you get from broken pieces. The flavor was clean and mild, which is what you want — pine nuts shouldn't dominate a basil pesto, they should round it out.

The one thing I can't verify is **long-term freshness**. Pine nuts have a high oil content that goes rancid in warm conditions. The resealable bag is good, but I'd still recommend transferring them to an airtight container in the fridge if you're not going through them within a month.

## Where it falls short

This is where I have to be direct with you. **The listing doesn't state the country of origin.** That's a red flag for pine nuts specifically. Chinese pine nuts are cheaper but often have a metallic, bitter aftertaste that lingers. Mediterranean (particularly Italian) and Korean pine nuts are considered superior but cost significantly more. At this price point, I'd guess you're getting a blend or a lower-cost source.

Also, the **price per ounce is likely higher** than buying pine nuts in bulk from a warehouse store. You're paying for the convenience of Amazon delivery and the kosher certification. If you're making pesto in large batches, this gets expensive fast.

## Who should buy this

- **Kosher-observant cooks** who need certified pine nuts and can't find them locally
- **Small households** that use pine nuts occasionally for pesto or salads and don't want a giant bulk bag going stale
- **Anyone who values whole nuts** over broken pieces for even toasting and better texture

**Skip it if** you're making large batches weekly — buy bulk instead. And if the lack of origin info bothers you, email the seller before ordering.

## FAQ

**Q: Are these pine nuts actually raw?**
Yes. The product is labeled raw and the ingredient list is just pine nuts — no oil, salt, or processing aids.

**Q: How should I store them to keep them fresh?**
Refrigerate in an airtight container. They'll last 2-3 months refrigerated, or up to a year frozen. The resealable bag works short-term but isn't ideal for long storage.

**Q: Can I use these for pesto and baking?**
Absolutely. That's their best use. Toast them lightly for baking or use raw for pesto — both work well with whole nuts.
