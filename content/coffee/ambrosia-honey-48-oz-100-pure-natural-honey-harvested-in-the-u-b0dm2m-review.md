**What's in the jug**

The 48 oz plastic jar arrives sealed with a tamper-evident lid. You're getting about 3 pounds of honey — roughly 6 cups — which for daily coffee drinkers means a 6-to-8 week supply. The honey itself is a light amber color with a mild, floral sweetness. It's pasteurized but not ultra-filtered, so you still get some natural pollen and enzymes. No corn syrup, no glucose syrup, no artificial flavors. The label screams "100% Pure" and the lab reports customers have posted back that claim up.

**Real-world performance in coffee**

Here's where it matters: how does it actually taste in a cup of black coffee? The flavor profile is gentle — think clover blossom rather than buckwheat's molasses punch. It dissolves readily in hot coffee (stir for 10-15 seconds) and leaves a clean, sweet finish without the metallic aftertaste some cheaper honeys have. For cold brew or iced coffee, it needs a quick stir or it'll sink to the bottom — a minor annoyance, not a dealbreaker. One reviewer nailed it: *"It's the closest thing to what my grandpa's bees produced. Sweet, not cloying, and it doesn't fight with the coffee notes."*

**Where it falls short**

Honest talk — this isn't perfect. The honey crystallizes fast. Within 3-4 weeks at room temperature, you'll see graininess forming. It's not spoiled; it's natural. But you'll need to set the jar in warm water for 10 minutes to bring it back to liquid. The plastic container is another gripe — honey is acidic over time, and glass is better for long-term storage. And the wide mouth means honey drips down the side when you pour, making a sticky mess on your counter unless you're careful.
