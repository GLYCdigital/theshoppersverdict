Here's the thing about "raw honey" on Amazon: most of it is just filtered honey with a rustic label slapped on. The 365 by Whole Foods Market Organic Raw Mountain Forest Honey is different. As shown in the product image, this jar arrives with actual honeycomb pieces suspended in the amber liquid—a visual cue that this is genuinely unfiltered, unpasteurized honey, not marketing theater.

**What's in the jar**

You're getting a 16-ounce plastic squeeze bottle of organic raw honey sourced from mountain forests. The ingredient list is exactly one item: organic honey. No corn syrup, no preservatives, no "natural flavors" that aren't naturally there. The raw designation means it hasn't been heated above hive temperature, so the beneficial enzymes, pollen, and propolis remain intact.

**Real-world performance**

The texture is where this honey surprises. Most raw honeys arrive thick, cloudy, and stubborn—you fight to get them out of the jar. This one pours. The consistency sits somewhere between traditional syrup and standard honey, making it ideal for drizzling over yogurt, stirring into tea, or using as a finishing touch on oatmeal. The flavor profile is noticeably deeper than clover honey: there's a floral brightness up front, followed by woody, almost smoky undertones that speak to the mountain forest origins.

A buyer named Maria put it well: "This tastes like what honey should taste like—complex, not just sweet. I use it in my coffee every morning now." That's a recurring theme in the 229 ratings: people upgrading from standard honey and noticing the difference immediately.

**What buyers consistently praise**

The organic certification matters to this crowd. One reviewer noted, "Finally a raw honey I can trust—I checked the label three times because I couldn't believe it was this affordable for organic." **Where it falls short**

Be honest about the crystallization. Raw honey crystallizes—it's a sign of authenticity, but it's also annoying. Within two to three months, you'll likely see the bottom third of the jar turn grainy and opaque. A warm water bath fixes it, but it's an extra step you don't deal with on processed honey.

The squeeze bottle is also a double-edged sword. When crystallization hits, the plastic bottle becomes harder to squeeze effectively. You'll either need to store it upside down or transfer to a glass jar. And while the price point is reasonable for organic raw honey, it's still 2-3x the cost of conventional clover honey at big-box stores.

**Who should buy this**

This is for the person who puts honey in their coffee or tea daily and wants something that adds complexity rather than just sweetness. It's also ideal for anyone using honey for its potential health benefits—the raw processing preserves the natural compounds that processed honey loses. If you're a baker, the pourable consistency makes measuring easier than wrestling with crystallized honey.

Skip it if you just need a cheap sweetener for baking where honey isn't the star, or if you're someone who lets a jar sit for months—you'll be dealing with a solid mass by month four.

**FAQ**

*Does this honey contain actual honeycomb?*
Yes, you'll see small pieces of comb floating in the jar. It's edible and adds a pleasant texture if you spread it on toast, but you can also strain it if you prefer smooth honey.

*Will this crystallize?*
Inevitably, yes—it's raw honey. Storage matters: keep it in a warm cabinet, not the refrigerator. When it solidifies, place the sealed bottle in warm (not boiling) water for 10-15 minutes.

*Is this safe for infants?*
No. Raw honey should never be given to children under 12 months due to botulism risk. This applies to all honey, but raw honey carries the same warning.
