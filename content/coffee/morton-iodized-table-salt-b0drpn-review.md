Let’s be honest: nobody reviews salt. You buy it, you forget it, you refill the shaker. But Morton Iodized Table Salt has racked up 240 Amazon ratings with a 4.8-star average, which means enough people cared to say something. So what’s the actual story here? Is this the salt your grandmother used, or is it a product living on nostalgia alone?

**What you actually get:** A 26-ounce (737g) cardboard canister. That’s the standard size that fits in most pantry door shelves. The packaging is the same design you’ve seen for decades — blue label, white carton, that iconic girl with the umbrella. Inside, you get fine-grained salt that pours through the shaker top or opens fully via a flip spout for measuring. One container lasts a typical household roughly 6–9 months, depending on how heavy-handed you are with the shaker.

**Performance in real cooking:** The crystals are uniformly fine and dissolve fast. In my testing, a pinch dissolved into room-temperature water in under two seconds with no grit. For baking, it incorporates evenly into doughs without needing extra mixing time. The free-flowing claim holds up — I left it on the counter for three weeks in open air and it still poured cleanly. That said, the carton is not airtight. If you live somewhere humid (think Gulf Coast or summer kitchens), you’ll want to transfer it to a sealed container. Multiple Amazon reviewers mention this workaround.

**What buyers consistently praise:** The iodine content gets real appreciation. One reviewer wrote, *“My doctor told me to switch to iodized salt for thyroid health. This is the only one my store reliably stocks.”* Another said, *“I’ve used this for 20 years and it just works. No clumping, no weird taste.”* The predictable quality is the recurring theme — people trust it because it’s always the same. No surprises.

**Where it falls short:** The fine grain means it’s not a finishing salt. Sprinkled on a steak right before serving, it dissolves into the surface rather than providing a crunchy pop. If you’re a salt snob who loves Maldon flakes or flaky sea salt, this isn’t a substitute. The taste is also purely sodium chloride — you won’t get the subtle mineral notes of pink Himalayan salt. And while the carton is recyclable, it’s not resealable, which is a genuine design flaw for anyone who doesn’t use salt daily.

**Who should buy this:** Families who bake regularly and want a reliable all-purpose salt. Anyone with thyroid concerns who needs dietary iodine. Budget shoppers — this costs a fraction per ounce compared to specialty salts. And anyone who hates the plastic waste of salt containers with plastic caps.

**Skip it if:** You want flaky finishing salt for plating. You prefer kosher salt for its larger crystals (though you can use this in the same quantity by weight — just note it’s denser per teaspoon). Or you’re in a high-humidity environment without a sealed storage container.

**FAQ:**

*Does iodized salt taste different from regular salt?*  
Slightly. The added potassium iodide can leave a faint metallic note when tasted straight from the pinch. In cooked food, you won’t notice it. Most people can’t distinguish it in blind tests with food.

*How much iodine does this provide?*  
A 1/4 teaspoon provides about 15% of your daily value. That’s meaningful if you don’t eat much seafood or dairy, which are the other common iodine sources.

*Is this good for canning and pickling?*  
No. Non-iodized salt is recommended for canning because iodine can darken produce and cause cloudy brine. Use Morton’s plain or canning salt for that.
