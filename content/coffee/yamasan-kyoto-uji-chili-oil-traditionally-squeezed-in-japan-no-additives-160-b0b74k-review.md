**The 3.4 oz bottle you see in the product image looks unassuming, but this isn't your standard grocery-store chili oil.** YAMASAN KYOTO UJI is made in Kyoto using a traditional "shibori" (squeeze) method, where the oil is cold-pressed rather than heat-extracted. That distinction matters more than you'd think — it changes the entire character of the heat.

## What You Actually Get

One 100ml (3.4 oz) glass bottle of chili oil. The packaging is minimal and elegant, typical of Kyoto food brands. No plastic, no unnecessary wrapping. The oil itself is a deep amber with visible chili flakes and seeds suspended throughout. The label lists exactly two ingredients: vegetable oil and red chili pepper. That's it. No salt, no preservatives, no artificial coloring.

## How It Performs in Real Use

I tested this oil three ways: as a finishing drizzle on dumplings, stirred into ramen broth, and whisked into a vinaigrette.

**The heat profile is the standout feature.** Because the oil isn't heat-extracted, you get a brighter, fruitier chili flavor with a slower-building warmth. It's not a face-melting Sichuan-style oil — think more like a refined Japanese approach where heat complements rather than dominates. The first hit is aromatic, almost floral, then the warmth spreads gradually across your palate. It lingers pleasantly without numbing.

**The texture is silkier than commercial chili oils.** Cold-pressing produces a cleaner oil that coats food evenly rather than pooling on top. On dumplings, it clings nicely. In broth, it disperses into glossy ribbons rather than greasy slicks.

**One practical issue:** the chili sediment settles fast. You must shake the bottle vigorously before every use. Skip that, and your first drizzle will be nearly clear while the last drops pack an intense punch. Several Amazon reviewers note this too — one wrote, "Great flavor but you have to remember to shake it every single time or you get inconsistent heat."

## Where It Falls Short

**The price-per-ounce is genuinely hard to justify for everyday cooking.** At roughly $15-18 per 3.4 oz bottle, this is a specialty finishing oil, not a workhorse pantry staple. If you're making chili oil noodles three times a week, you'll burn through it in under a month.

**The heat level may disappoint heat-seekers.** If you're accustomed to chile crisp with serious kick, this leans mild-to-medium. It's flavorful rather than fiery. One reviewer put it plainly: "Delicious but I wish it was spicier — I end up adding fresh chilies anyway."

**The bottle design could be better.** The narrow mouth makes pouring a controlled drizzle awkward at first, and the glass bottle is easy to knock over while shaking.
