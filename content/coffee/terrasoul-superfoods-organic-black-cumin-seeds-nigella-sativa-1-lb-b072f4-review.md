## How it performs in real use

I toasted a tablespoon in a dry pan for about 90 seconds and the transformation is immediate — the raw, somewhat musty aroma becomes nutty and savory, almost like a cross between oregano and toasted onion. Crushed over a salad with olive oil and lemon, it adds a bitter, herbal bite that's genuinely pleasant. In bread dough, it behaves like poppy seeds but with more personality.

That said, grinding is where this product earns or loses its keep. Nigella seeds are tiny and tough. My electric blade grinder produced an uneven powder with visible chunks after 30 seconds. If you're planning to use these for tea or supplements, you'll want a proper spice mill or a mortar and pestle. Plan for an extra five minutes of elbow work.

The aroma is aggressive. If you're not familiar with nigella's pungent, almost sulfuric edge, the first whiff might smell less like "superfood" and more like "something went bad in the pantry." It fades once toasted or cooked, but raw applications can be a shock. Also, this is a whole-seed product, not a ground powder. If you want convenience, you'll need to put in the grinding work yourself. Finally, the pouch is functional but not airtight — I transferred mine to a glass jar for long-term storage, and you should too.

## FAQ

**Can I eat these raw?**
Yes, but they're bitter and intensely aromatic. Toasting for 60-90 seconds in a dry pan mellows the flavor significantly and releases more of the nutty character.

**How do I store them?**
Keep them in the resealable pouch in a cool, dark place — or transfer to an airtight glass jar. They'll hold potency for 6-12 months. Refrigeration extends that but watch for moisture.

**Are these the same as regular cumin seeds?**
No. Despite the name, Nigella sativa is unrelated to Cuminum cyminum (true cumin). The flavor profile is different — more oregano-onion than warm and earthy.
