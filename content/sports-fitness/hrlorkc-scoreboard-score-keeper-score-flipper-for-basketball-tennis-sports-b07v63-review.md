## What You Actually Get

The package includes three separate score panels: one for the home team, one for the visiting team, and a third that doubles as a period/innings counter. Each panel measures roughly 16 inches tall and 7 inches wide — compact enough to sit on a scorer's table without dominating it, but big enough that the numbers do the talking.

The numbers themselves are the headline feature. At 5.5 inches tall, they're visible from across a standard high school gym. The cards are black on one side and red on the other, giving you high-contrast readability even under harsh gym lighting. The frame is metal, not cheap plastic, and the binding rings holding the cards feel substantial — these won't pop open mid-game.

## Real-World Performance

I've flipped a lot of scorecards in my time, and the HRLORKC has a specific feel. The cards are thick cardstock with a laminated finish, which means they're durable but slightly stiff right out of the box. A few buyers noted this — one reviewer said, "The cards are a bit tight when you first get them, but after a few games they loosen up and flip easily." That matches my experience. Give it a quick break-in session at home before your first real game, and you'll avoid mid-game fumbling.

The ring mechanism is where this product earns its keep. The rings are welded, not glued, and they hold the cards securely while still allowing smooth rotation. I've seen cheaper scorekeepers where the rings bend after a few uses, leaving cards hanging at awkward angles. Not here. The construction feels like it was designed for a season of abuse, not a single afternoon.

## Where It Falls Short

No product is perfect, and the HRLORKC has a few honest drawbacks. The lack of a carrying case is the most obvious miss. The cards can get scuffed or bent if you toss the panels loose into a duffel bag. A simple drawstring pouch would have solved this — as it stands, you'll want to wrap them in a towel or buy a separate case.

The stiffness issue I mentioned earlier is worth repeating. Some buyers found the cards "hard to flip at first" and noted that younger scorekeepers (think 10-12 year olds) might struggle until the cards break in. It's a minor issue that resolves itself, but it's worth knowing before you hand this to a kid on game day.
