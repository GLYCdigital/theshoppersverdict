## What's Actually in the Box

You get three medals—one each for 1st, 2nd, and 3rd place—with color-coded ribbons already attached. The dies are stamped from zinc alloy, and each medal measures about 2.5 inches in diameter. That's a standard competition size, not the oversized "participation trophy" look you sometimes see at corporate events.

The ribbons are the differentiator here. First place gets red/white/blue, second gets red/white, and third gets blue/white. It's a small detail, but it means someone can identify placement from across a gymnasium without squinting at the medal itself.

## Build Quality and Real-World Performance

The zinc alloy construction gives these medals a genuine heft—roughly 1.5 ounces each. When you hand one to a 10-year-old who just won their first race, that weight matters. It signals "real award," not "party favor." The front face has a raised star motif with enamel fill, and the back is flat enough for engraving if you want to add names or dates.

Here's the tradeoff: the "gold" and "silver" are electroplated finishes over the base alloy, not solid precious metal. That's expected at this price point, but it means the finish will wear at the edges with heavy use. One reviewer noted, "After a full season of weekly youth track meets, the gold plating is starting to show bronze underneath." That's not a defect—it's physics. If you're running a single event, they'll look pristine. If you're doing year-round programming, budget for replacements.

## What Buyers Keep Praising

The consistency of feedback is striking. Multiple reviewers mention the same thing: these look more expensive than they are. One parent wrote, "My son thought they were real Olympic medals until he saw the price." Another confirmed, "The weight is perfect—not flimsy, not so heavy they drag the ribbon down."

The color coding also gets consistent shout-outs. "No more confusion at the awards table. The kids know exactly which is which before they even read the print," said a coach who ordered three sets for a school field day.

## Where It Falls Short

The ribbons are the weakest link. They're functional but thin—about 1 inch wide with a stitched edge that feels like it could separate if a kid swings the medal around by the ribbon (which they will). For a one-off event, fine. For a weekly league, you'll want to replace them with something sturdier.

Also, the packaging is utilitarian—a simple poly bag. If you're gifting these as a coach's end-of-season gesture, you'll want to buy a display box separately. And while the back is engraveable, the surface is slightly textured, so engraved text won't be as crisp as on a perfectly smooth plate.

## FAQ: Three Questions Shoppers Actually Ask

**Can these be engraved?**
Yes. The back has a recessed panel designed for engraving. A local trophy shop can do it, or you can use a handheld engraver. Just note the surface texture isn't glass-smooth, so fine script won't be as sharp as on a high-end plaque.

**Are they safe for young children?**
The medals are metal with a ribbon—no small parts, no sharp edges. However, the plating contains trace metals, so if you have a child who mouths everything, keep these for kids 5 and up.

**How do they compare to plastic medals?**
These are categorically better. The weight and metallic finish are what make them read as "official." Plastic medals scream "cheap" the moment someone picks them up. If you're charging an entry fee for your event, these justify it.
