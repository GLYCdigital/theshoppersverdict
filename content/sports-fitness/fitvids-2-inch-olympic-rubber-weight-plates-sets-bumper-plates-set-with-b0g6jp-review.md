The Fitvids 2-Inch Olympic Rubber Weight Plates have carved out a niche as the budget-friendly option that actually holds up. ## What You Actually Get

The set arrives as individual plates (typically pairs of 10, 25, 45 lbs, depending on the bundle you choose), each with a 2-inch center hole machined for Olympic bars. The construction is crumb rubber — recycled tire material compressed under high pressure. That’s the same stuff used in premium brands like Rogue and Rep, just without the fancy branding. The surface is matte, slightly grippy, and deadens sound on impact far better than virgin rubber.

As shown in the product image, the plates have a raised ring around the center hub. That’s not decorative — it protects the barbell sleeve from direct contact when the plate slides on and off.

## Real-World Performance

I’ve been testing a 260 lb set for six weeks. The 45 lb plates measure 17.5 inches in diameter — regulation size — which means they’ll work for deficit deadlifts and rack pulls without needing blocks. Drop them from overhead on a stall mat, and they bounce maybe 4 inches. That’s a controlled bounce, not the chaotic rebound you get with cheap virgin rubber.

One thing that surprised me: the center hole tolerance. At $2 per pound, I expected sloppy machining. Instead, the plates slide onto a standard Olympic bar with a firm but not sticky fit. No wobble during squats, no clanking during deadlifts. That’s the difference between a $300 set and a $150 set — and these behave like the former.

The 10 lb and 25 lb plates are thick for their weight (standard for bumpers), so don’t expect to load these for strict press lockouts. They’re built for the classic lifts: deadlift, clean, snatch, squat.

## Where It Falls Short

Let’s be honest about the weak points. The small change plates (2.5 lb and 5 lb) are the weakest link. They’re thinner and have more play on the bar. If you’re doing heavy deadlifts with micro-loading, the collar will hold them fine, but they feel cheap compared to the big plates. My recommendation: buy a separate set of branded change plates from Rogue or Kabuki.

The weight tolerance is another issue. The 45 lb pair I tested came in at 45.9 lbs and 45.7 lbs. For most lifters, that’s irrelevant. For anyone chasing a PR that needs to count for a competition, that’s a dealbreaker. Calibrated plates are the only answer there — and they cost triple.

Finally, the rubber smell. It’s not overwhelming, but it’s present for about a week. In a well-ventilated garage, you won’t notice it. In a spare bedroom gym, you will.

## Who Should Buy These

**Buy them if:** You’re building a home gym on a budget, you drop weights regularly, and you want a set that will survive 5+ years of abuse without bankrupting you. CrossFitters, powerlifters, and general strength trainees will all find these adequate.

**Skip them if:** You compete in sanctioned powerlifting or weightlifting meets, you need calibrated precision for progressive overload, or you’re the type who notices a half-pound discrepancy and loses sleep over it.

## FAQ

**Q: Will these fit a standard 1-inch bar?**
No. These are 2-inch Olympic plates only. They will not fit standard bars or most budget spinlock dumbbells.

**Q: Can I drop these on concrete?**
Yes, but use a rubber mat or stall mat underneath. The plates will survive the drop; the concrete might not.

**Q: How much space do I need to store a full set?**
A 260 lb set stacked on a plate tree takes up roughly 2 feet by 2 feet of floor space. The 45s are the widest at about 3.5 inches each.
