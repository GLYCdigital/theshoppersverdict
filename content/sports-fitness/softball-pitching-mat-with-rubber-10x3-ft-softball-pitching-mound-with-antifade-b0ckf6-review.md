This is a 10-foot by 3-foot rubber mat with a molded pitching rubber integrated into one end. The dimensions matter — a standard 43-foot pitching distance means your drive foot will land well past the 3-foot mark if you have a proper stride. The anti-fade claim isn't marketing fluff. Multiple buyers noted the white pitching rubber stays visible even after months of cleat contact. That's critical — if you can't see the rubber, you can't hit your spots in practice.

Here's where this mat separates itself from cheaper alternatives. It's heavy. Not "annoying to move" heavy, but "stays put in a gust" heavy. ## Where It Falls Short

Let's be honest about the downsides. **There's no carrying handle.** At this size and weight, you'll be wrestling it to and from your car. It's doable, but a strap would've been an easy win.

**Wet traction is a concern.** A few buyers reported the surface getting slick when shoes are damp. If you practice in dewy mornings or after rain, wipe your cleats before stepping on. This isn't a dealbreaker, but it's worth knowing.

**It's not a full mound replacement.** This mat gives you a flat landing surface with a pitching rubber — it doesn't replicate the elevation change of a real mound. For pitchers who train exclusively on flat ground, transitioning to a mound still requires adjustment.

## Who Should Buy This

## FAQ

**Will this fit in a standard car trunk?**
Rolled up, it's roughly 3 feet long and 10 inches in diameter. It fits in most sedan trunks, but you'll want to secure it so it doesn't roll around.

**How do I clean it?**
Hose it down and let it dry. Avoid harsh chemicals — they can degrade the rubber over time. A quick brush with a stiff broom handles dirt and debris.

**Does it work on concrete?**
Yes, but consider adding a non-slip underlayment. The mat itself is stable, but concrete can be unforgiving on joints — you might want a thin foam pad underneath for extra shock absorption.
