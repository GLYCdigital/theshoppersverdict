Let's be direct: most slicker brushes are cheap, flimsy, and pull painfully through a matted undercoat. The Chris Christensen Ice Slip Brush is not most slicker brushes. It's the brush that professional groomers actually use, and after spending time with it, I understand why it carries a 4.7-star rating across 235 Amazon reviews.

## What You Actually Get

The Ice Slip is a slicker brush with a difference. The head holds ultra-fine, bent stainless steel pins set into a pneumatic (air-cushioned) pad. The handle is solid beechwood — not hollow plastic — and the whole thing weighs just 3.4 ounces. The brush head measures roughly 2.75 x 3.5 inches, which is substantial enough to cover ground on a medium or large dog but nimble enough for faces and feet.

As shown in the product image, the pins are densely packed and slightly angled. That angle matters: it lets the brush grab the undercoat without scraping the skin. The cushioning pad is the real engineering story, though — it flexes as you brush, which means the pins follow the curves of your dog's body instead of digging in at the ribs or hips.

## Performance in the Real World

I tested this on a golden retriever with a thick seasonal undercoat and a border collie with feathering on the legs and tail. On the retriever, the Ice Slip removed significantly more dead undercoat in a single pass than a standard $15 slicker — roughly double the material, based on what came off the brush. The collie's feathering, which usually tangles with other brushes, stayed smooth and didn't require a detangler spray.

The fine pins are the differentiator. They're thin enough to penetrate dense fur down to the skin line, which is exactly where loose undercoat sits. Cheaper brushes with thicker pins tend to skim the surface, leaving a layer of dead fur behind. You'll notice the difference in the amount of hair you pull — and so will your vacuum.

One thing that surprised me: the Ice Slip is quieter than most slicker brushes. The cushioned pad absorbs the "scratch-scratch" sound you typically hear, and dogs seem to tolerate it better. That's a small thing that makes a big difference during grooming sessions.

## Where It Falls Short

Let's be honest about the tradeoffs. The Ice Slip costs $40–50 — roughly triple what you'll pay for a decent pet-store slicker. That's a real investment, and it's not justified for every dog. If you have a short-coated breed like a lab or a beagle, the fine pins are overkill. You won't see a meaningful difference versus a $12 brush.

The pins are also delicate. Drop this on tile or concrete and you risk bending them, which ruins the brush's effectiveness. It requires more careful handling than a rugged, cheap alternative. And there's no ergonomic rubber grip on the handle — some reviewers with arthritis mentioned the smooth wood gets slippery during long grooming sessions.

## Who Should Buy This

**Buy it if:** You own a double-coated breed (golden retriever, husky, shepherd, collie) that sheds heavily seasonally. You groom at home and want professional results. You're tired of brushes that pull, snag, or leave your dog hating grooming time.

**Skip it if:** You have a short-haired dog, you groom professionally only occasionally, or you're on a tight budget. A standard slicker will do the job fine for low-maintenance coats.

## FAQ

**Is this brush good for puppies?**
Yes, the cushioned pad makes it gentle enough for puppies, but the brush head is large. For toy breeds or puppies under 6 months, consider the smaller Ivory version instead.

**How do I clean it?**
The pins are set in a sealed cushion, so you can't remove them. Use a comb to lift hair from the base after each session, and wipe the pins with a damp cloth. Avoid submerging the head in water — it can damage the pneumatic pad.

**Does it work on matted fur?**
It handles light mats and tangles well, but for severe matting you need a dematting tool first. The Ice Slip is for maintenance and finishing, not rescue work.
