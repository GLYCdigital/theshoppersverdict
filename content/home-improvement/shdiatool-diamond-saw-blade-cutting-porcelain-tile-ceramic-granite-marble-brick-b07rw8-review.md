Some blades promise a lot and deliver mediocre chips. The SHDIATOOL diamond saw blade sits at a 4.3-star average across 215 ratings, which tells you it does something right — but the reviews also reveal exactly where it won't save you. Let's get into what this blade actually does on porcelain, ceramic, granite, and the brick it claims to handle.

**What you get out of the box**

The package is straightforward: one 4.5-inch continuous-rim diamond blade with a 7/8-inch arbor (the standard M14 for angle grinders). It's a turbo rim design — that means a serrated edge with diamond segments brazed on, not a smooth continuous rim like you'd see on a glass blade. The construction is a single-piece steel core with vacuum-brazed diamond grit. Nothing fancy, but the brazing quality is what separates a $15 blade from a $50 one, and this holds up reasonably well.

**Real-world performance**

I tested this on three materials: rectified porcelain (the hard stuff), glazed ceramic, and a chunk of granite scrap. On a wet saw with steady water flow, this blade cuts porcelain cleanly. The turbo segments grab the material and pull through at a consistent rate — no grabbing, no bouncing. On 12x24 rectified tile, I got chip-free cuts along the glaze, which is the main thing buyers care about when they're paying $4 per square foot for tile.

On granite, it's slower but acceptable. You'll want to take shallow passes — about 1/8 inch per pass — and let the blade do the work. Pushing harder just generates heat and wears the diamonds faster.

**Where buyers consistently praise it**

The common thread in positive reviews is the value-to-performance ratio. One buyer said, "Cuts porcelain like butter, no chipping at all. I've used blades twice the price that didn't perform this well." Another noted, "Used on my angle grinder with a straightedge guide. Clean cuts on 3/8-inch porcelain. Would buy again." The consensus is clear: for the price point, this blade outperforms expectations on porcelain and ceramic.

**Where it falls short — be honest with yourself**

Here's the reality check. This is not a dry-cutting blade for thick porcelain. Without water, it will glaze over, slow down, and leave burn marks on the tile edge. If you're doing a backsplash with thin ceramic, you can get away with short dry cuts — but for floor tile, you need a wet saw or a spray bottle and patience.

The 4.5-inch diameter also limits your depth of cut to about 1.5 inches. For standard 12x24 porcelain that's 3/8-inch thick, you're fine. But if you're stacking tile or cutting thick pavers, you'll need to flip the tile and cut from both sides — a hassle.

The arbor is 7/8-inch, which fits most angle grinders, but if you have a 1-inch arbor saw, you'll need a reducer ring. Check your tool before ordering.

**Who should buy this**

- **DIYers and tile installers** doing porcelain or ceramic on a budget — this is your blade.
- **Contractors** who need a disposable blade for job sites where losing a $15 blade is fine.
- **Anyone cutting granite or marble countertop edges** with a grinder and a guide — it'll work, just go slow.

**Skip it if**

- You need a dry-cutting blade for thick porcelain without water. Look for a segmented turbo blade with cooling holes instead.
- You're cutting through thick stone slabs in one pass. This isn't the tool.
- You want a 7-inch or 10-inch blade for a table saw. This is strictly angle-grinder territory.

**FAQ**

**Q: Can this blade cut porcelain tile without chipping?**
A: Yes, when used on a wet saw or with water spray. Dry cutting on porcelain will cause chipping at the glaze edge. Keep water flowing and use a steady feed rate.

**Q: Does the 4.5-inch blade fit a standard angle grinder?**
A: Yes, it has a 7/8-inch arbor (M14), which is the standard for most 4.5-inch angle grinders. Check your grinder's arbor size — some DeWalt models use a 5/8-inch arbor and will need a reducer.

**Q: How long does the blade last?**
A: On porcelain, expect roughly 300-500 linear feet of cutting before the diamonds wear noticeably. On harder granite, that drops to 150-200 feet. It's a consumable — treat it that way.
