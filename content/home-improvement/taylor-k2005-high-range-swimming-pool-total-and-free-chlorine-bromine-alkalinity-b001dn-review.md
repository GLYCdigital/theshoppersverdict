## What You Actually Get

The K2005 is a FAS-DPD titration kit, which is a fancy way of saying you count drops instead of matching colors. Inside the box you'll find:

- **R-0870 DPD indicator powder** (free chlorine reagent)
- **R-0871 titrating reagent** (the drops you count)
- **R-0002 acid/base indicator** for total chlorine
- **R-0013 cyanuric acid reagent**
- **R-0014 alkalinity indicator**
- Two clear viewing tubes with caps
- A laminated instruction card (worth keeping — you'll reference it)

The 0.75 oz bottles are smaller than the professional K2006 kit, but for a typical residential pool, they'll last a season of weekly testing. The comparator block is solid, not flimsy plastic.

## Performance and Real-World Use

Here's where the K2005 separates itself from every test strip on the shelf. The FAS-DPD method uses a powdered reagent that turns your water sample pink, then you add drops until it turns clear. Each drop equals 0.2 ppm of chlorine. That precision matters — especially if you're dealing with a green pool or trying to maintain a salt system at exact levels.

The critical advantage is measuring **free chlorine separately from total chlorine**. The difference between those two numbers is combined chlorine — chloramines — the stuff that makes your pool smell like bleach and burns your eyes. Test strips can't show you this. One Amazon reviewer put it plainly: *"I thought my pool was fine based on strips. This kit showed I had a chloramine problem I'd been fighting for weeks."*

The process takes about 5 minutes for a full panel of tests. You'll need to count drops carefully — this isn't a kit you can use while distracted by kids jumping in the pool. But the accuracy is worth the extra minute.

## Where It Falls Short

Let's be honest about the tradeoffs. **There's no pH test in this kit.** For a complete picture, you'll need the Taylor K1004 or a separate pH test. That's a notable omission for a kit at this price point.

The drop-counting method requires steady hands and good eyes. If you're testing at dusk or in bright sun, reading the exact moment the color changes can be tricky. One reviewer noted, *"My wife can't use this — she has trouble seeing the color change and worries she's adding too many drops."*

And here's the long-term cost reality: replacement reagent bottles (R-0870 and R-0871) run about $25-$30 for a set. Over two seasons, you'll spend more on reagents than the kit itself.

## FAQ

**Q: How often do the reagents need replacing?**
A: The 0.75 oz bottles typically last 1-2 seasons with weekly testing. The DPD powder (R-0870) has a longer shelf life than liquid reagents — store it in a cool, dry place.

**Q: Can I use this for a spa or hot tub?**
A: Yes, but the high-range chlorine test works best for pools. For hot tubs with higher chlorine demands, consider the Taylor K-2006 which has larger bottles.

**Q: Why does my pool smell like chlorine even though my free chlorine reading is fine?**
A: That smell is chloramines — combined chlorine. The K2005's total chlorine test will reveal this. If total chlorine is 0.5 ppm or more above free chlorine, you need to shock.
