If you've ever stripped a laptop screw or over-tightened a tiny hinge, you already know why a torque screwdriver earns its place in your drawer. The coobeast Torque Screwdriver (around $30) promises precision without the $100+ price tag of iFixit or Wera kits. After digging through 229 Amazon ratings and the specs, here's the honest breakdown.

## What You Actually Get

The package includes the screwdriver body, a 60-bit set (CR-V steel, ranging from #000 Phillips to T10 Torx), and a hard-shell case with clearly labeled bit slots. The tool itself measures about 7 inches long — short enough for tight spots, long enough for comfortable grip. The torque dial sits at the base, and you twist to select one of 10 presets (2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 in-lb). A small window shows your selected value, which is genuinely useful mid-task.

## Performance and Build Quality

The aluminum body is the first thing you notice. It's cold to the touch, knurled for grip, and weighs noticeably more than plastic precision drivers. That weight helps — you get better control at low torque settings. The magnetic bit holder keeps bits seated securely, though one reviewer noted, "The magnet is strong enough for the bit, but not so strong that it picks up loose screws from the tray. That's actually a plus once you realize it."

In real use, the torque mechanism clicks audibly when you hit the limit. The click is distinct — not a vague "did I feel something?" moment. Users consistently report the settings are accurate when tested against known torque values. One verified buyer wrote: "I've used it on MacBook bottom screws, drone motors, and a camera rig. Every time I set it to the spec, it clicks exactly when it should. No stripped heads in three months."

The bit quality is decent for the price. They're not Wiha-grade, but they fit snugly in screws without wobble. The case has a latch that stays closed in a toolbox, and the bit slots are molded tightly enough that bits don't rattle loose.

## What Buyers Consistently Praise

Across the reviews, three themes dominate:

1. **Accuracy at low torque** — Multiple reviewers use it for electronics repair (laptops, phones, RC vehicles) and trust the 2–4 in-lb settings.
2. **Value for money** — A common sentiment: "This replaced a $60 precision set that didn't even have torque control."
3. **The case** — People actually like the storage. It's compact (about 4 x 3 x 1.5 inches) and fits in a backpack pocket.

## Where It Falls Short

The biggest limitation is the preset torque values. You get 10 fixed settings, not a continuous dial. If your spec calls for 3.5 in-lb, you're choosing between 3 and 4. For most hobby work that's fine, but it's a dealbreaker for professional repair techs who need exact values.

Second, the bit holder is magnetic, not a precision collet. It works, but if you're working overhead or in awkward angles, a small bit can pop out. One reviewer mentioned: "Dropped a T6 bit inside a printer while working upside down. Not the tool's fault, but a collet would've prevented it."

Also, the 2 in-lb minimum means this isn't ideal for micro-screws on tiny electronics (like watch straps or eyeglass hinges). For that, you'd want a screwdriver starting at 0.5 in-lb.

## Who Should Buy This

- **Laptop/phone repair hobbyists** — The 2–10 in-lb range covers most consumer electronics specs.
- **RC and drone builders** — Propeller and motor screws are exactly where this tool shines.
- **DIYers who've stripped one too many screws** — The click feedback prevents over-torquing before you learn "the feel."

Skip it if you're a professional repair tech needing calibrated, continuous torque values — you'll want a CDI or Wera model at 3x the price.

**Q: Can I use it for eyeglass repair?**
