A door closer is one of those products you don't think about until the door slams at 2 a.m. or a gust of wind rips it out of your hand. The LYNN HARDWARE Extra Heavy Duty Automatic Door Closer is aimed at the person who's tired of flimsy residential units and wants something that actually holds up. At 4.7 stars from 235 ratings, it's clearly doing something right. Here's the real story.

## What You Actually Get

The box contains the closer body, a forged steel main arm, a sliding shoe assembly, and all the mounting hardware — screws, bolts, and a template for drilling. The unit itself is cast aluminum with a corrosion-resistant finish, rated for doors up to 250 pounds and widths up to 48 inches. That's solidly in commercial territory.

What stands out immediately is the adjustable closing and latching speed. Two hex valves on the face of the unit let you dial in how fast the door closes and how firmly it latches. You don't need a degree in hydraulics to use them — turn clockwise to slow, counterclockwise to speed up. Simple.

## Real-World Performance

I've installed a lot of door closers, and the first thing I check is whether the adjustment valves actually do anything. On cheap units, they're decorative. On this LYNN HARDWARE unit, they work. Crank the latch valve and you'll feel the difference in the last few inches of travel — the door positively clicks shut without slamming.

The closing speed valve handles the main sweep. Set it too fast and the door moves like it's angry; back it off and you get a smooth, controlled close. There's enough range here to handle both a light interior door and a heavy exterior one.

The universal mounting is a genuine plus. You can install it on the push side (regular arm), pull side (top jamb), or use the parallel arm configuration for limited clearance applications. That's three installation styles from one box, which saves you from buying the wrong type.

## Where It Falls Short

Let's be honest about the downsides. The included hardware is serviceable but not premium. If you're mounting into a steel frame, you'll likely want to swap the self-tapping screws for machine screws with proper anchors. It's a five-minute trip to the hardware store, but it's still an extra step.

More notably, this unit lacks a backcheck adjustment valve. Backcheck controls how much resistance the closer provides when the door is pushed open hard or caught by wind. Without it, a strong gust can still slam the door if you haven't tuned the main speed valve carefully. For most residential and light commercial use, it's a non-issue. For a door exposed to constant wind, it's a real limitation.

Some buyers also note the arm assembly requires careful alignment during installation. It's not difficult, but it's not a five-minute job either. Budget 30-45 minutes for your first install.

## FAQ

**Q: Will this fit my door?**
A: Check the door weight and width. The unit handles up to 250 lbs and 48 inches wide. For standard residential doors (typically 80 lbs and 36 inches), it's overkill — which is fine, it just means the closer will outlast the door.

**Q: Can I install this myself?**
A: Yes, if you're comfortable with a drill and a screwdriver. The included template helps with alignment. Allow about 30-45 minutes. If you're mounting into metal, grab proper anchors first.

**Q: How do I adjust the closing speed?**
A: Two hex valves on the closer body. The one closer to the door hinge adjusts latching speed; the other controls closing speed. Turn clockwise to slow down, counterclockwise to speed up. Make small quarter-turn adjustments and test each time.
