Let's be direct about this faucet: it's not trying to be a $200 designer piece. It's a workhorse bathroom faucet that nails the basics, gives you real installation flexibility, and doesn't fall apart after six months. Here's the breakdown.

## What You Actually Get

Out of the box, you've got the faucet body with pre-attached supply lines, a metal deck plate for converting a 3-hole sink to a single hole setup, mounting hardware, and an Allen wrench. No drain assembly, no extra frills. The brushed nickel finish on the model I tested looks clean and resists fingerprints better than polished chrome, though darker finishes do show hard water spots.

The headline feature here is the 1 or 3 hole compatibility. If you're replacing an old faucet on a 3-hole countertop, you don't need to buy a separate deck plate or patch holes — it's included. That's a genuine money-saver and the plate sits flush enough that it doesn't look like an obvious retrofit.

## Build Quality and Real-World Performance

The body is solid brass, not the zinc alloy or plastic you see at this price point. That matters for longevity — brass handles the thermal cycling of hot and cold water without degrading. The ceramic disc cartridge is the same type found in faucets costing three times as much, and it shows in the handle action. Movement is smooth, with no grinding or sticking.

Water flow is consistent and the aerator does its job without excessive splashing. Temperature mixing is predictable — you get a good range of adjustment rather than the touchy "all hot or all cold" problem that plagues cheap cartridges. One thing worth noting: if your home has low water pressure, this faucet still performs acceptably, which isn't universally true at this price.

## Where It Falls Short

The pre-attached supply lines are the biggest practical annoyance. At roughly 14 inches, they're fine for shallow vanities, but if you have a deeper cabinet or a wall-mount shutoff that sits low, you'll need extensions. That's an extra trip to the hardware store and an additional $10-15.

The missing pop-up drain is the other gotcha. If you're replacing an existing faucet and reusing the old drain, you're fine. But if you're doing a full renovation or the old drain is corroded, budget for a separate one — this faucet doesn't include it.

And a final honest note: the finish, while attractive, requires upkeep. Hard water leaves spots, and on darker finishes they're more visible. A microfiber wipe every couple of weeks keeps it looking sharp, but if you want zero-maintenance, consider brushed stainless instead.

## Who Should Buy This

This is ideal for three types of buyers:

- **DIY homeowners** replacing an old faucet on an existing 3-hole countertop — the included deck plate eliminates extra parts and hassle
- **Landlords or property managers** who need reliable fixtures that won't generate service calls
- **Budget-conscious renovators** who want a solid brass faucet without paying designer markup

Skip it if you need a matching pop-up drain, have a deep vanity requiring longer supply lines, or want a high-arc design for filling tall containers.

## FAQ

**Will the deck plate fit my existing 3-hole sink?**
The plate measures about 7 inches across, which covers standard 4-inch or 6-inch center-set configurations. If your holes are spread wider than that, measure first — it won't cover an 8-inch spread.

**Are the supply lines long enough?**
They're pre-attached at about 14 inches. For most standard vanities, yes. For deeper cabinets or awkward shutoff placement, budget for extensions.

**Does this work with tankless water heaters or low pressure?**
Yes. The ceramic cartridge and aerator handle low-flow situations well, and several reviewers with tankless systems reported no issues with temperature consistency.
