If you've ever tried to block-sand a quarter panel with a palm sander, you already know the frustration: uneven pressure, skipped spots, and fingers cramping after ten minutes. The Ingersoll Rand 315G straight line air sander exists to eliminate that pain. This is a dedicated body-shop tool built for one job — long, flat, straight-line sanding — and it does that job exceptionally well.

## What You're Actually Getting

The 315G arrives as a bare tool, no case, no pads. You get the sander itself: a 2.75" x 17.5" composite platen mounted on a straight-line (inline) drive mechanism. The footprint is substantial — this isn't a detail sander, it's a panel-length tool. The design puts the air inlet at the rear, with a textured grip area running the full length of the body. Controls are minimal: a feathering dial on the side and a trigger throttle underneath.

The 10,000 RPM free speed is the headline spec. But the real story is the feathering control — a dial that lets you adjust the amount of air reaching the motor, effectively letting you vary sanding pressure without changing your hand position. That's the feature that separates this from cheaper straight-line sanders.

## Performance and Real-World Use

I've spent hours with this sander on everything from primer to clear coat. The straight-line action is smooth — no orbital swirl marks, just clean, directional sanding that's perfect for feathering paint edges before a respray. The feathering dial works as advertised: dial it back for light final passes, open it up for aggressive material removal. It's intuitive and, once you find your setting, repeatable.

The composite body stays cool even after twenty minutes of continuous use. Weight is manageable at roughly 3.5 pounds. Vibration is present but well-damped for a tool in this class — your hands will feel it after a full day, but not the pins-and-needles numbness you get from cheap units.

One thing to note: this is a wet-sanding tool. There's no dust port, no bag, no shroud. If you're dry-sanding, you'll be cleaning up a mess. Most body-shop users run it wet, which keeps the paper loaded and the dust down.

## Where It Falls Short

The 315G demands air. It needs 5 CFM at 90 PSI minimum, and realistically you want more headroom. If you're running a 6-gallon pancake compressor, this sander will starve and stall. You need at least a 20-gallon tank, ideally bigger.

The lack of dust collection is a genuine limitation for dry-sanding applications. If you're planning to use this in a garage without running water, you'll be dealing with significant airborne dust.

The feathering dial is a bit stiff initially — it loosens up with use, but expect a learning curve. And the low-profile body, while great for control, means your knuckles are close to the work surface. Thin gloves only.

## Who Should Buy This

- **Collision repair techs** who need a reliable, repeatable straight-line sander for daily use. This is a tool that survives shop life.

Skip it if you're a casual DIYer with a small compressor, or if you only sand occasionally and don't want to deal with wet-sanding setup and cleanup.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I use this for dry sanding?**
A: Technically yes, but there's no dust collection. You'll be creating a significant mess. It's designed for wet sanding — the water keeps paper loaded and controls dust. For dry use, look at the orbital version or add a shop vac setup manually.

**Q: What size compressor do I need?**
A: Minimum 5 CFM at 90 PSI, but that's the floor. Realistically, you want a compressor that can deliver that continuously — think 20-gallon tank or larger. Small hobbyist compressors will cause the sander to bog down mid-pass.

**Q: Does it come with sandpaper or a backing pad?**
A: No. It's a bare tool. You'll need to buy adhesive-backed sandpaper sheets cut to the 2.75" x 17.5" platen size. Many users cut standard 9" x 11" sheets to fit, but pre-cut rolls are available from body shop suppliers.
