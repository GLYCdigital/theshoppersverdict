Let's cut through the noise: the zozen Chainsaw Mill is a $120-ish answer to a $400 problem. It won't replace a bandsaw mill, but for turning storm-felled oak into live-edge slabs or squaring up beams for a shed, it does the job with fewer headaches than the price suggests. With 220 ratings averaging 4.4 stars, the consensus is clear—this thing works, but it has quirks you need to know before you bolt it to your saw.

## What's Actually in the Box

Unboxing reveals the full kit: the main mill frame, two 18-inch guide rails, a crossbar assembly, and all necessary bolts, washers, and Allen keys. There's also a small instruction sheet—rudimentary, but adequate. The frame is welded square-tube steel with a powder-coat finish that resists chipping better than I expected. The guide rails are heavy-gauge aluminum with pre-drilled holes that line up cleanly with most consumer chainsaw bars (Stihl, Husqvarna, Echo, and the ubiquitous Amazon-brand bars). No missing hardware, no stripped threads out of the box. That alone puts it ahead of several competitors in this price band.

## Performance: Where It Shines and Where It Struggles

I ran this mill on a 20-inch Stihl MS391 with a ripping chain, cutting white oak and pine. The first thing you notice is the rigidity. The square-tube frame doesn't flex like the thinner models from other budget brands—cuts stay true even when you're leaning into the saw. Depth adjustment is tool-free: loosen two knobs, slide the frame, lock it down. The laser-etched markings are accurate to about 1/16 inch, which is plenty for rough lumber.

The included guide rails are a genuine weak point, though. They're functional, but they flex slightly on cuts longer than 10 feet. If you're milling 8-foot logs, you'll want to brace the rail ends with a scrap 2x4. Buyers in the reviews echo this: one user noted, "The rails are decent but you definitely need to shim them on longer logs to keep the cut straight." That's honest feedback, not a dealbreaker.

## What Buyers Consistently Praise

The overwhelming majority of positive reviews center on value and ease of setup. A verified buyer wrote: "First time using a chainsaw mill. Had it assembled in 20 minutes and was cutting slabs by the afternoon. The depth adjustment is way easier than my buddy's Granberg." Another praised the sturdiness: "I've milled about 300 board feet with this and it hasn't bent or warped. Solid for the money." The tool-free depth adjustment gets repeated mentions—it's a genuine time-saver compared to wrenches and locknuts on pricier units.

## Where It Falls Short

No product is perfect, and the zozen has three honest flaws. First, the clamp bolts that secure the mill to the bar can strip if you reef on them. Use a torque wrench set to spec, or at least stop when you feel resistance—it's a soft metal. Second, there's no oiler bracket included. For cuts longer than 4 feet, you'll want a remote oiler or you'll burn up your chain. Third, the 24-inch max bar length is limiting. If you're milling 30-inch-diameter logs, look elsewhere. This is a small-to-medium log tool.

## Who Should Buy This

You're the target buyer if you own a chainsaw between 18 and 24 inches, want to mill your own slabs for furniture or siding, and don't want to drop $400+ on a Granberg. It's ideal for homesteaders, hobbyist woodworkers, and anyone clearing timber and wanting usable lumber instead of firewood. Skip it if you're a professional sawyer or plan to mill logs over 18 inches in diameter regularly—you'll outgrow it in a season.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I use it with a 16-inch chainsaw?**
A: Yes, but you'll be limited to roughly 12-inch-wide slabs. The frame adjusts down, though the included rails are sized for 18-inch bars.

**Q: Does it work with electric chainsaws?**
A: It'll bolt on, but electric saws lack the torque for sustained milling. Stick with gas-powered saws 50cc and up.

**Q: How long does assembly take?**
A: About 15-20 minutes with basic hand tools. The instructions are sparse, but the design is intuitive.

## The Verdict

**Buy it if** you want a budget-friendly entry into chainsaw milling for occasional slabs and beams on logs up to 16 inches in diameter. **Skip it if** you're milling professionally or need to handle larger timber—the rail flex and bar limit will frustrate you.

**Rating: 4.2/5** — It's not perfect, but at this price, it's the best balance of rigidity, adjustability, and cost you'll find. Pair it with a ripping chain and an auxiliary oiler, and you'll be cutting dimensional lumber for pennies on the dollar.
