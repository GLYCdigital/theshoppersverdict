If you've ever watched water seep past a window frame or felt a draft whistle through a door gap, you know the stakes. A failed seal isn't just annoying — it's rot, mold, and energy loss. GE's Advanced Silicone for Window & Door claims to be the last tube you'll need for exterior waterproofing. After digging through 235 ratings averaging 4.7 stars, the consensus is clear: this is a serious performer, but it demands respect during application.

## What You Actually Get

The tube is standard 9.8-ounce caulk size, fitting any standard caulk gun. This is 100% silicone sealant — not a hybrid or latex blend. That distinction matters. It's formulated specifically for non-porous substrates: glass, aluminum, vinyl, fiberglass, and cured paint. The "Advanced" tag refers to improved flexibility and adhesion compared to older GE silicone formulas. You get one tube, no applicator tip included — you'll need your own caulk gun and a utility knife to cut the nozzle.

## Real-World Performance

Here's where this product separates itself from the bargain-bin stuff. The sealant cures to a rubbery, flexible consistency that handles thermal expansion without cracking. On exterior window seams exposed to direct rain and UV, buyers report seals holding for multiple seasons without yellowing or separating. One reviewer noted, "Sealed my basement window wells in spring — still watertight after a hurricane-level downpour in August."

The adhesion on glass is exceptional. Silicone bonds chemically to glass surfaces, creating a waterproof barrier that latex caulk simply cannot match. For aluminum door frames or vinyl window flanges — surfaces that repel other adhesives — this holds firm.

Tooling is workable. You get roughly five to ten minutes of working time before the skin forms. Smooth it with a wet finger or a dedicated tool for a clean, professional bead. Full cure takes 24 hours, and it's rain-ready after that.

## What Buyers Consistently Praise

The dominant theme in positive reviews is reliability. "This is the only caulk I trust for exterior work now," wrote one five-star reviewer. Another praised its durability: "Applied in 90-degree heat, survived a winter of ice and snow — zero cracks." The 100% waterproof claim isn't marketing fluff — it's a genuine silicone that doesn't absorb water or break down in wet environments.

## Where It Falls Short

This is not a one-tube-fits-all solution. The biggest complaint — and it's legitimate — is that it's **not paintable**. Silicone rejects paint outright. If you need a color-matched seam on visible trim, you're stuck with the glossy translucent or white finish. Mask off adjacent surfaces meticulously before applying, because once it's on, it's on.

The acetic acid cure (that vinegar smell) is another real issue. It dissipates in a day or two, but in a closed room it's unpleasant. Ventilate aggressively or avoid using it in occupied indoor spaces.

Removal is the third strike. If you make a mistake, you're in for a fight. Silicone doesn't dissolve in water or most solvents — it requires scraping, silicone-remover chemicals, and elbow grease. This is a "measure twice, cut once" product.

## Who Should Buy This

- **Homeowners sealing exterior windows and doors** — this is the primary use case and it excels there
- **DIYers replacing old, cracked silicone** around tubs, showers, and sinks that need genuine waterproofing
- **Contractors working with non-porous materials** like aluminum, glass, and vinyl who need a dependable sealant

**Skip it if** you're caulking drywall, wood trim, or any surface you plan to paint. You'll need a paintable latex or hybrid sealant instead.

## FAQ

**How long does it take to cure?**
Skin forms in about 10 minutes; full cure takes 24 hours. Don't expose it to water or stress the joint during that window.

**Can I use this indoors?**
Yes, but the vinegar odor during curing is strong. Ventilate the space for 24-48 hours. It's safe once cured.

**Does it work on wet surfaces?**
No. The surface must be clean, dry, and free of grease or old caulk residue. Silicone will not bond to a wet substrate.
