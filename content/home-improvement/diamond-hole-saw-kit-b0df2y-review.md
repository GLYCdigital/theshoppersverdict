If you’ve ever tried to drill through porcelain tile with a standard carbide bit, you know the drill: it skates, it squeals, and it leaves a cracked tile that costs you $8 to replace. That’s exactly the frustration this diamond hole saw kit is designed to eliminate — and based on the 4.6-star average across 229 ratings, it’s doing the job for most buyers. But let’s be clear: this is a tool with a learning curve, and it rewards patience over brute force.

## What You Actually Get

The kit ships with a range of sintered diamond hole saws — typically covering 6mm to 50mm (roughly ¼-inch to 2-inch) — plus the mandrels and guide pins needed to mount them on a standard drill. Sintered construction means the diamond grit is fused into the metal matrix, not just electroplated onto the surface. That distinction matters: sintered bits cut longer and handle harder materials without shedding their abrasive layer after a few uses.

As shown in the product image, the bits have a hollow barrel design with a smooth shank that fits standard 3/8-inch chucks. The included mandrels are threaded and swap out quickly, which is convenient when you’re moving between hole sizes mid-project.

## Performance and Real-World Use

Here’s where this kit earns its keep. On porcelain tile — the worst-case scenario for most drill bits — these saws cut through with steady, consistent pressure. One reviewer noted, “Drilled 20 holes in 12x24 porcelain tile with zero chips. Used a spray bottle to keep water flowing and it went through like butter.” That’s the experience most buyers report: clean, round holes without the blowout you get from hammer drills or carbide-tipped bits.

The key is water. Every serious user emphasizes this. The diamond edge generates heat that will fracture the bit if you run it dry. A spray bottle, a wet sponge, or even a trickle from a garden hose makes the difference between a bit that lasts through dozens of holes and one that dies on the third.

Glass, ceramic, and stone all respond well to this kit. It’s less ideal for metal — you’d want a bi-metal hole saw for steel — but for bathroom remodels, kitchen backsplashes, and shower installations, this is the right tool.

## Where It Falls Short

Be honest with yourself: this kit has a learning curve. There’s no pilot bit, so starting a hole on a smooth tile surface requires a guide. You can tape a scrap of wood or a piece of cardboard over the mark, or use the included guide pin on softer materials. On vertical surfaces, that angled start is the most common place for the bit to walk and scratch the tile.

The other limitation is speed. This is not a tool for impatient people. Running the drill at high speed or applying heavy pressure will glaze the diamond surface and ruin the bit. The correct approach — moderate RPM, steady light pressure, constant water — takes longer per hole than a carbide bit, but the results are far superior.

## Who Should Buy This

**Buy it if** you’re a DIYer tackling a tiling project, a plumber running supply lines through ceramic, or an electrician cutting fixture holes in porcelain. It’s also a solid choice for hobbyists working with glass bottles or stone.

**Skip it if** you only need one hole, you’re working exclusively in wood or drywall, or you’re unwilling to keep water flowing while you drill. If that’s the case, a $10 carbide bit is the cheaper admission to the frustration club.

## FAQ

**Q: Can these bits cut tempered glass?**
No. Tempered glass is designed to shatter, and no drill bit will change that. Use this kit on annealed glass, tile, and stone — not tempered products.

**Q: How fast should I run the drill?**
Keep it under 1,500 RPM. High speed creates heat that kills the diamond bond. Let the bit do the work with light pressure.

**Q: Will these fit my cordless drill?**
Yes, the shanks are standard 3/8-inch, so any drill with a keyed or keyless chuck will accept them. Cordless drills work fine, though a corded model maintains more consistent speed under load.
