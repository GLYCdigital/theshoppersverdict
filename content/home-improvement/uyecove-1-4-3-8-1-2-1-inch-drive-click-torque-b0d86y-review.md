Let me be straight with you: buying torque wrenches individually gets expensive fast. A decent 1/2-inch drive unit alone runs $60–$100. This UYECOVE set puts four wrenches in one case for roughly the price of two mid-range singles. That's the headline.

## What you actually get

The kit includes four click-type torque wrenches: a 1/4-inch drive (2–24 Nm / 1.5–17.7 ft-lb), a 3/8-inch drive (5–80 Nm / 3.7–59 ft-lb), a 1/2-inch drive (20–210 Nm / 14.8–154.9 ft-lb), and the big one — a 1-inch drive (135–815 Nm / 100–600 ft-lb). All four come in a rigid blow-molded case with cutouts for each tool. The wrenches have dual-scale laser-etched markings on the knurled adjustment collars, which is easier to read than stamped numbers on cheaper tools.

The shafts are chrome vanadium steel with a satin finish. The ratchet heads have a 72-tooth mechanism, giving you 5-degree increments for working in tight spaces. Extension bars come included for the 1/4 and 3/8 drives.

## Real-world performance

The click mechanism is where budget torque wrenches live or die, and this one performs well above its price point. The click is distinct — you get both an audible snap and a tactile bump through the handle. I've used $20 wrenches where the click was so weak you'd overshoot by 10 ft-lb before noticing. That's not a problem here.

One buyer noted: *"I checked the 1/2-inch drive against my Snap-on at 80 ft-lb and it clicked within 1 ft-lb. Not bad for a set that costs less than my lunch."* Another verified the 3/8-inch unit against a calibrated beam wrench and found it within 3% at mid-range.

The 1-inch drive wrench is the outlier here. It's a heavy-duty unit rated for 100–600 ft-lb, which is truck and tractor territory. The range is so wide that a full turn of the adjustment collar moves the setting significantly — you won't be fine-tuning to the nearest foot-pound. For lug nuts on a 3/4-ton truck, that's fine. For precision work, you'd want a smaller-range tool anyway.

## Where it falls short

Let's be honest about the limitations. First, the 1-inch wrench doesn't have a reversible ratchet head — it's fixed direction. Second, there's no calibration certificate in the box, so you're trusting the factory settings. Most buyers report accuracy within spec, but a certified unit costs more. Third, the storage case is functional but not rugged — it'll survive a garage shelf but not a truck bed.

The 1/4-inch drive covers a low range (2–24 Nm), which is good for small fasteners, but the click is less pronounced at the bottom of its range. If you're torquing tiny electronics screws, you might prefer a beam-style wrench for that application.

## Who should buy this

- **Home mechanics** working on cars, motorcycles, or ATVs who need multiple drive sizes without buying four separate tools

## FAQ

**Q: Is the accuracy good enough for engine work?**
A: Yes, for typical automotive applications. Multiple buyers report checking these against known-good wrenches and getting results within 2–4%. That's fine for spark plugs, caliper brackets, and suspension components. Just don't trust it for critical fasteners without a calibration check.

**Q: Does the 1-inch drive come with a socket?**
A: No, it's just the wrench. You'll need to buy a 1-inch drive socket separately, which can be pricey. The 1/4 and 3/8 drives include extension bars, but no sockets are bundled.

**Q: How do I set the torque?**
A: Loosen the locking collar, rotate the knurled handle to the desired setting using the scale markings, then tighten the collar. The scales show both ft-lb and Nm, with the primary unit depending on the wrench. The 1-inch drive is marked in ft-lb.
