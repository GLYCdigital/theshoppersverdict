If you're replacing a leaky shut-off valve behind a toilet or under a sink, you've probably stared at a wall of options at the hardware store and wondered if the $8 special will fail in two years. The GUOFIS compression stop valve sits at a higher price point — around $30 — but the 4.8-star average across 240 ratings suggests buyers aren't regretting the upgrade. ## What You Actually Get

The valve is machined from solid brass with a polished chrome finish — the kind of build that feels substantial in hand. The inlet is a 1/2-inch nominal compression fitting (that's 5/8-inch outside diameter for the copper tube), and the outlet steps down to 3/8-inch OD, which is the standard size for toilet supply lines and most faucet hoses. You get the valve body and a plastic quarter-turn handle. That's it — no mounting escutcheon, no mounting hardware. The compression nut and ferrule are pre-installed on the inlet, which saves a step.

## Real-World Performance

Installation is straightforward if you've done compression fittings before. Slide the nut and ferrule onto the copper pipe, push the valve on, and tighten. The key is snugging the nut — the valve seals via the ferrule compressing onto the pipe. The quarter-turn handle has a distinct stop at both ends, so there's no ambiguity about whether you've shut off water flow. Multiple buyers noted this precision, with one writing, "The handle clicks into position with authority — you know it's closed." Another reviewer who installed six of these across a whole-house repipe said, "Every single one sealed on the first try. No Teflon tape needed on the compression side."

The chrome finish holds up well in damp environments. Unlike cheaper zinc valves that develop white corrosion spots within months, buyers report the GUOFIS still looks clean after a year in service.

## Where It Falls Short

Let's be honest about the tradeoffs. The plastic handle is the most obvious cost-saving measure. It's functional and won't crack under normal use, but if you're matching fixtures in a high-end bathroom, it'll look a bit utilitarian. Also, there's no escutcheon plate in the box. That means the rough hole in your wall or floor will be visible unless you buy a trim ring separately — a small extra expense and trip to the store.

One more consideration: at roughly $30 per valve, replacing every shut-off in a house gets pricey. If you're doing a budget remodel with five or six valves, that's $150–$180 just for stops.

## FAQ

**Q: Does this valve fit standard 1/2-inch copper pipe?**
A: Yes. "1/2-inch nominal" refers to the pipe's inside diameter. The compression fitting is sized for 5/8-inch outside diameter, which is what standard 1/2-inch copper tubing measures. It will not fit 1/2-inch OD PEX or CPVC.

**Q: Do I need to solder this or use pipe dope?**
A: No soldering required. Compression fittings seal mechanically — just tighten the nut. No pipe dope or Teflon tape is needed on the compression threads, though a bit on the outlet threads (if connecting to a threaded supply line) doesn't hurt.

**Q: Is the 3/8-inch outlet compatible with standard toilet supply lines?**
A: Yes. The 3/8-inch OD outlet is the industry standard for toilet and faucet supply hoses. If your existing hose has a 3/8-inch compression or threaded connection, it'll fit.
