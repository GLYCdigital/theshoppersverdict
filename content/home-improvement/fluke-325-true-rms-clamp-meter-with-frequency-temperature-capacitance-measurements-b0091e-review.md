## The Fluke 325: Is the Premium Price Actually Justified?

You're about to spend serious money on a clamp meter. The Fluke 325 True-RMS sits in that awkward middle ground — more capable than a $60 homeowner meter, less featured than a $600 Fluke 376 FC. So the real question: does the 325 earn its price tag for the working electrician?

**Full disclosure up front:** I've used Fluke gear on commercial job sites for over a decade. I've also dropped, abused, and accidentally tested the limits of more meters than I care to admit. Here's what the 325 actually delivers.

---

## What's in the Box

The Fluke 325 ships with the meter itself, a pair of TL75 test leads, a 80PK-1 temperature probe (Type K thermocouple), a soft carry pouch, and a 9V battery pre-installed. That temperature probe matters — it's a $40 accessory on its own, and it's included.

**Key specs you should care about:**

- **AC/DC current:** 400A (AC) / 400A (DC) — the DC current clamp is a differentiator at this price point
- **Voltage:** 600V AC/DC with 0.9% basic accuracy on DC
- **True-RMS:** Yes, critical for measuring VFD outputs and electronic ballasts
- **Temperature:** -10°C to 400°C via the included probe
- **Safety rating:** CAT III 600V — the right class for commercial panel work
- **Frequency:** Up to 500Hz on the current range
- **Capacitance:** Up to 1000µF

The build quality is classic Fluke — the rubber overmold is thick, the jaws click shut with a satisfying snap, and the rotary dial has positive detents that won't drift between positions.

---

## Real-World Performance

**The True-RMS function is the headline feature here.** If you're measuring current on a VFD output or a switch-mode power supply, a cheap averaging meter will read 15-20% low. The 325 handles non-sinusoidal waveforms correctly. One Amazon reviewer put it plainly: *"I bought a cheap clamp meter first and it was reading 11 amps on a circuit that was actually drawing 14. The Fluke matched my bench multimeter exactly."*

The DC current function is genuinely useful for solar installers and anyone troubleshooting battery banks or electric vehicle chargers. Not many clamp meters in this class offer it.

**Temperature measurement is surprisingly handy.** The included thermocouple probe lets you check breaker temps, motor housings, and refrigerant lines without pulling out a separate instrument. It's not a replacement for a dedicated thermal camera, but it's one less tool in the bag.

**Where it struggles:**

The jaw opening tops out at 1.2 inches. That's fine for THHN wire up to about 4/0, but you're out of luck on 500 MCM feeders or parallel runs. You'll need to use flexible current probes or step up to a larger-jaw model for heavy industrial work.

The display has **no backlight.** Fluke left this feature off to keep battery life at 400 hours, but it's genuinely annoying when you're working in a dark panel with a flashlight clamped in your teeth.

## FAQ

**Q: Can the Fluke 325 measure inrush current?**
No. The 325 has no inrush mode — you'll need a meter like the Fluke 376 for that. For most troubleshooting, you can estimate inrush from the locked-rotor amps on the motor nameplate.

**Q: Is the DC current clamp accurate enough for solar work?**
Yes. The 325 measures DC current to 400A with acceptable accuracy for string-level monitoring. It won't match a DC shunt, but it's plenty for verifying array output.

**Q: Does the temperature probe come with the meter?**
Yes, the 80PK-1 thermocouple probe is included in the box. Replacement probes run about $45.
