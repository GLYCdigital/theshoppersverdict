If you own an RV, you already know the roof is a ticking clock. Every seam, vent flange, and antenna base is a potential entry point for water — and water is the one thing that will total your rig faster than a fender bender. The Dicor 501LSW-1 self-leveling lap sealant is the product most RV technicians grab first, and after testing it across two seasons, I understand why.

## What You Actually Get

This is a single 10.1-ounce tube of self-leveling sealant, not a caulk gun cartridge. The squeeze tube design matters — you cut the tip at a 45-degree angle, puncture the inner seal, and lay a bead directly onto the roof surface. The product is formulated specifically for TPO and EPDM rubber roofs, which covers roughly 90% of travel trailers and motorhomes built in the last two decades. It's also paintable, which matters if you care about aesthetics on a white roof.

## Performance and Real-World Application

The "self-leveling" label isn't marketing hype. Within 60 seconds of laying a bead, the sealant flows outward to fill hairline cracks and gaps around screw heads and vent flanges. You do not need to tool it with a putty knife — gravity does the work. On a sloped roof, it will find its way into gaps you didn't even know were there.

I applied this to a 2019 Keystone Passport with a TPO roof that had developed cracks around the bathroom vent flange. The old sealant had pulled away from the rubber, leaving a gap you could slide a credit card into. After cleaning the area with isopropyl alcohol and letting it dry completely, I laid a single bead. The sealant flowed into the gap within seconds and skinned over in about 15 minutes. Full cure took roughly 24 hours at 70°F.

The bond is aggressive. After two months of highway driving and one hailstorm, the sealant showed zero signs of peeling or separation. The product spec claims a service temperature range of -20°F to 200°F, and while I didn't test the extremes, it remained flexible through a Minnesota winter without cracking — which is more than I can say for the factory sealant that failed originally.

## Where It Falls Short

This sealant has a temperature floor. Below 40°F, it won't flow properly and may never fully cure. If you're doing a winter repair, you need to heat the tube and the surface — or wait for a warm day. That's a genuine limitation for northern RV owners.

The 15-minute skin-over time is also a double-edged sword. It means you can't go back and smooth out mistakes. If you lay a crooked bead, you're scraping it off and starting over. For beginners, this can waste a tube or two while you learn the technique.

Finally, this is a maintenance product, not a permanent fix. Expect to reapply every 12-18 months depending on sun exposure and roof flex. That's normal for lap sealants, but buyers should know going in.

## Who Should Buy This

This is for the hands-on RV owner who does their own roof inspections and isn't afraid of a ladder. Skip it if you're a full-time RVer in a cold climate doing winter repairs, or if you have a fiberglass roof (you need a different product — Dicor makes a non-self-leveling version for vertical surfaces that would work better for your application).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long does one tube last?**
At a standard 1/4-inch bead, one 10.1-ounce tube covers about 25 linear feet. For most RVs, that's enough to do all roof penetrations — vents, antenna, AC base — in a single pass. Buy two tubes if you want a second coat or have a large fifth wheel.

**Can I apply this over old sealant?**
Yes, but only if the old sealant is clean, dry, and firmly adhered. If it's cracked or peeling, remove it completely with a plastic scraper before applying. Dicor makes a primer (503) for heavily weathered roofs that improves adhesion on marginal surfaces.

**Is this safe for fiberglass roofs?**
No. This is formulated for TPO and EPDM rubber roofs only. On fiberglass, it won't bond properly and will peel within months. Use a polyurethane sealant instead.
