## What You Actually Get

The box contains exactly one tool: a 12-inch (305mm) set of pliers with a slide-adjustment mechanism. No case, no gloves, no nonsense. The jaws open from roughly 2.5 inches up to 5 inches, which covers nearly every spin-on filter on the market — from a lawnmower's tiny cartridge to a diesel truck's oversized canister. The adjustment lock is a single thumb lever that releases the jaw position; it's simple, mechanical, and doesn't rely on a ratcheting mechanism that can slip under load.

The construction is where WORKPRO shows its hand. The jaws are heat-treated carbon steel with a black oxide finish, and the handles wear a dual-layer grip that stays put even when coated in oil. The pivot pin is solid, and there's no lateral play when you squeeze — a common failure point on cheaper adjustable pliers.

## Real-World Performance: The Grip Test

Here's the thing about oil filter pliers: torque means nothing if the jaws slip. I tested these on a genuinely stuck filter — 2017 Honda CR-V, 8,000 miles overdue, filter welded on by heat and time. The WORKPRO grabbed the filter's rolled edge on the first bite. The 12-inch handles gave enough leverage to break it loose with one hard pull, and the jaws didn't deform or skip. That's the whole ballgame.

The infinite adjustment range is a genuine advantage over fixed-size strap wrenches. You can reset the jaw width mid-pull without releasing pressure, which matters when you're working blind under a car. The grip pattern — a crosshatch tooth design — bites into the filter's metal without crushing it. You'll likely get the filter off intact, which is more than I can say for channel-lock pliers that dent the can and shear the drain plug.

## Where It Falls Short

Let's be straight about the limitations. The straight-handle design is a problem in modern engine bays. On my Subaru Outback, the filter sits behind the exhaust manifold — there's no room to swing a 12-inch handle. You'll need a swivel-head or a socket-style filter wrench for those applications. This is a garage tool, not an "everything" tool.

Second, the jaw teeth are aggressive. If you're using an aftermarket anodized or painted filter (like a K&N), expect cosmetic marring where the jaws bite. It won't damage the threads or the sealing surface, but it will scratch the finish. Not a function issue — just a reality of grip-based removal.

Finally, the adjustment lever can collect grime. After a few dirty jobs, you'll want to wipe it clean or the slide gets sticky. It's a minor maintenance point, not a dealbreaker.

## Who Should Buy This

**Buy it if:** You do your own oil changes on cars, trucks, or motorcycles, and you've had enough of strap wrenches that slip. This is also a solid pick for boat owners or anyone working on equipment with multiple filter sizes — the range means one tool covers the fleet.

**Q: Will this fit a standard Toyota/Chevy/Honda oil filter?**
