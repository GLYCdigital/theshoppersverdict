Let me cut through the noise: most stockpots under $100 are thin, flimsy aluminum that warps on medium heat. The Cooks Standard 18/10 Stainless Steel Stockpot is not that. With a 4.7-star average across 235 ratings, this pot has earned its reputation — but it's not perfect, and you should know exactly where it falls short before you commit.

## What You Actually Get

The stockpot features a tri-ply construction: a thick aluminum core sandwiched between an 18/10 stainless steel exterior and interior. The aluminum core is the workhorse here — it spreads heat evenly across the base, eliminating the hot spots that plague single-layer stainless. The pot comes with a matching stainless steel lid that seals well, plus two riveted side handles for lifting when full (and trust me, this pot gets heavy when loaded).

As shown in the product image, the design is clean and utilitarian — no gimmicks, no colored enamel, just brushed stainless that looks professional on any cooktop. It's available in multiple sizes, but the 8-quart and 12-quart versions are the most popular for batch cooking and canning.

## Performance: The Real Test

I've simmered a 6-hour bone broth, seared a 4-pound chuck roast before braising, and boiled pasta for a crowd. Here's what matters:

**Heat distribution:** The tri-ply base is genuinely impressive. A full pot of water comes to a rolling boil noticeably faster than on my cheaper stockpot, and more importantly, the heat stays even. No scorching on the bottom when I'm making a thick tomato-based sauce that needs low, steady heat.

**Oven versatility:** This is where the Cooks Standard punches above its price class. The entire pot — lid included — is oven-safe to 500°F. That means you can sear on the stovetop, then transfer directly to a 350°F oven for braising without dirtying a second vessel. That's a feature I'd expect on a $200 All-Clad, not a budget-friendly stockpot.

**Induction compatibility:** The encapsulated base works on all cooktop types, including induction. That's a non-negotiable for anyone who's bought "stainless" cookware only to discover it won't magnetically couple with their induction burner.

The reviews echo what I found in testing. One verified buyer writes: *"I've owned this for 6 months and it's still shiny — I use it 3-4 times a week. ## Where It Falls Short

Let me be direct about the downsides, because they're real.

**No pour spouts.** This is my biggest gripe. When you're draining a full pot of pasta or transferring stock, the rim is a straight edge — liquid pours unevenly and drips down the side. You'll need a colander for pasta and a ladle for stock. It's not a dealbreaker, but at this price point, pour spouts should be standard.

**Stainless requires maintenance.** The interior will show rainbow discoloration and water spots if you don't dry it immediately after washing. It's cosmetic, not functional, but it bugs some owners. A quick wipe with a towel after cleaning prevents 90% of the issue.

**Handles get warm.** The riveted side handles stay cooler than the pot body, but on a long, low simmer, they warm up enough to require a mitt. The lid knob is better, but I've still grabbed it too quickly once.

## FAQ

**Q: Can this go in the dishwasher?**
A: Technically yes, but don't. The manufacturer recommends hand washing, and dish detergent will cause discoloration and water spots on the stainless interior. Hand washing takes 30 seconds and keeps the pot looking new.

**Q: Does it work on induction cooktops?**
A: Yes. The encapsulated aluminum base includes a magnetic stainless layer, so it couples properly with induction burners. This is verified by both the manufacturer and multiple buyer reviews.

**Q: What sizes are available?**
A: The Cooks Standard line offers this stockpot in 4, 6, 8, and 12-quart versions. For most households, the 8-quart is the sweet spot — big enough for a whole chicken or a large batch of chili, but not so large it's awkward to store. The 12-quart is for serious canners or big-batch soup makers.
