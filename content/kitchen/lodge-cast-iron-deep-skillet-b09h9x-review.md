The Lodge Deep Skillet is the answer to a question you didn't know you had: what if a cast iron pan could also be a Dutch oven's lighter, more maneuverable cousin? At first glance, it looks like a standard 10.25-inch skillet that's been stretched taller. But that extra inch and a half of wall height changes everything about how you cook.

## What you actually get

Unboxing reveals a pre-seasoned pan — no gray factory coating, just the dark, matte finish that Lodge has been shipping for decades. It's a 3-quart skillet with a 10.25-inch cooking surface, which means you can sear a steak flat while still having room to braise short ribs in liquid up to the rim. The pan arrives ready to cook, straight from the box. No scrubbing off wax, no oven cycles at 400 degrees, no wondering if you did it right.

The design details matter here. Dual pour spouts on both sides let you drain bacon fat or pan sauce without dribbling down the side of the pan. A helper handle opposite the main one gives you a second grip point — trust me, you'll need it when this thing is full of stew.

## Performance and build quality

Cast iron's superpower is heat retention, and this pan has it in spades. Preheat it slowly and it will hold an even, steady temperature that sears better than any nonstick or stainless pan at three times the price. The deep walls trap splatter too — frying chicken no longer ends with a greasy stovetop.

The rough surface is the trade-off. Lodge doesn't machine-polish their pans like some boutique brands, so the cooking surface has a slightly textured feel. It's fine for meat, vegetables, and cornbread. Eggs, delicate fish fillets, or crepes will stick until you build up seasoning over months of regular use. This isn't a flaw — it's the nature of affordable cast iron. You're trading a little upfront nonstick performance for $40 instead of $150.

## Where it falls short

Let's be honest about the weight. This pan is heavy — nearly 7 pounds empty, and closer to 9 or 10 when filled with braising liquid. If you have wrist issues or prefer to flip and toss food, this isn't your pan. You'll use a spatula and forearm strength instead.

The rough finish is the second real drawback. For a brand-new pan, it's not as smooth as competitors like Victoria or Field Company. It will improve with use, but if you want slidey eggs on day one, buy a separate nonstick pan for that job.

One more thing: the pre-seasoning is functional but thin. It'll handle the basics fine, but I'd recommend doing a few oven-seasoning cycles in the first month to build a more robust base layer. It's 20 minutes of effort that pays off for years.

## FAQ

**Is this the same as a regular Lodge skillet?**
No. The 10.25-inch standard skillet is 2 inches deep with a 1.7-quart capacity. This one is 3 inches deep with a 3-quart capacity. That's a meaningful difference for soups, sauces, and anything that splatters.

**Can I use it on an induction cooktop?**
Yes. Cast iron is fully compatible with induction, gas, electric, and oven use up to 500°F. The lid handles are also oven-safe.

**How do I clean it?**
Skip the soap paranoia — modern dish soap is fine for cast iron. Use hot water and a stiff brush, dry thoroughly, and rub a thin layer of oil over the surface before storing. For stuck-on food, simmer water in the pan for a few minutes, then scrape.
