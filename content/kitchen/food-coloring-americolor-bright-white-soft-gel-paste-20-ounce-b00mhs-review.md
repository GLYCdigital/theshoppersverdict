If you've ever fought to get a true white buttercream—layering in titanium dioxide, crossing your fingers against that sickly yellow tint—you already know the struggle. AmeriColor's Bright White Soft Gel Paste is the professional baker's answer, and at $23 for 20 ounces, it's priced like a tool, not a toy. The question is whether it earns that price tag for home bakers. Short answer: mostly yes, with one packaging caveat.

## What You Actually Get

The 20-ounce tub is a serious quantity—roughly triple what hobbyist brands like Wilton sell in their small jars. The gel itself is thick, almost like a stiff toothpaste, with no drippy liquid separating out on the shelf. As shown in the product image, it's a squat, wide-mouth tub with a clear lid, which makes it easy to see how much you have left. There's no spoon or scoop included, so budget for a dedicated spatula if you don't want to contaminate your frosting with other colors.

## Real-World Performance

I tested this in both American buttercream and Swiss meringue. The pigment is genuinely concentrated. A half-teaspoon turned a cup of cream-colored frosting into a clean, bright white—no gray cast, no chalky residue. It also held its color after 48 hours at room temperature, which is a key test. Cheaper whites oxidize to a dull beige; this one stayed true.

One thing worth knowing: the gel doesn't dissolve instantly. You need about 60 seconds of vigorous mixing to get a uniform color. If you're in a rush and under-mix, you'll see white streaks in your final product. That's not a flaw—it's just physics—but it's worth factoring into your workflow.

## What Buyers Consistently Praise

The Amazon reviews, 240 strong at 4.8 stars, cluster around two themes. First, the pigment strength. ## Where It Falls Short

The packaging is the real weak point. The wide-mouth tub is easy to dip into, but the lid rim collects dried paste over time. When you open it, small flakes of hardened gel can fall into your frosting. It's not a dealbreaker, but you'll want to wipe the rim clean after every use.

There's also a texture issue if you overdo it. Past a certain threshold—roughly a full teaspoon per cup of frosting—the gel adds a slight chalky mouthfeel. It's subtle in American buttercream but more noticeable in lighter Swiss meringue. Use the minimum amount needed and adjust from there.

## Who Should Buy This

This is for the baker who does at least one decorated cake per month, or anyone who's tired of buying tiny tubes of white coloring that run out mid-project. If you're a casual baker doing one birthday cake a year, the 20-ounce size is overkill—you'd be better off with a smaller jar. But if you're making wedding cakes, sugar cookies, or any project requiring consistent white bases, this is the workhorse you want.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I use this in chocolate or candy melts?**
A: Not effectively. Gel colors are water-based, so they'll seize chocolate. Stick to oil-based colors for chocolate work.

**Q: How much do I actually need per batch?**
A: Start with 1/4 teaspoon per cup of frosting and add more if needed. A single 20-ounce tub can whiten roughly 80-100 cups of frosting.

**Q: Does it change the flavor?**
A: No. It's tasteless at normal usage rates. Only at extreme overuse (which you shouldn't do anyway) does it add a faint chemical note.

## The Verdict

**Buy it if** you bake regularly and want reliable, pure white frosting without the guesswork. **Skip it if** you're a once-a-year holiday baker or you primarily work with chocolate—you'll waste most of the tub.
