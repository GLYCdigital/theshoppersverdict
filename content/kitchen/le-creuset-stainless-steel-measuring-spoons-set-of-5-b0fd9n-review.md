## What's in the Box

The set includes five spoons: 1 tablespoon, 1 teaspoon, 1/2 teaspoon, 1/4 teaspoon, and 1/2 tablespoon. That's a slightly unusual lineup — most sets skip the 1/2 tablespoon and include an 1/8 teaspoon instead. Le Creuset chose the half-tablespoon, which is actually more useful for everyday cooking (it's the standard serving size for salad dressings and marinades).

Each spoon is stamped from a single piece of 18/10 stainless steel. That's the same grade used in high-end cookware — 18% chromium, 10% nickel — which means it won't react with acidic ingredients like tomatoes or citrus. The set arrives connected by a polished split ring, and there's also a separate ring for individual spoon storage if you prefer to hang them separately.

## Build Quality and Real-World Performance

Here's where the Le Creuset set separates itself from the bargain bin. The bowls are noticeably deeper and narrower than typical stamped spoons. That geometry matters: when you measure honey, molasses, or vanilla extract, the liquid sits inside the bowl rather than beading over the edges. A 1/4 teaspoon of vanilla actually stays in the spoon instead of dripping onto your counter.

The handles have a subtle weight to them — not heavy, but substantial enough that the spoons don't feel flimsy. The edges are smoothly rolled, so there are no sharp seams to catch on your fingers or scratch your mixing bowls. One Amazon reviewer noted, "I've had these for two years and run them through the dishwasher daily. They still look brand new — no spotting, no rust."

I'll echo that. The 18/10 steel resists the pitting that plagues cheaper 18/0 stainless, and the measurements are engraved — not painted — so they won't fade after repeated washings.

## What Buyers Consistently Praise

The overwhelming feedback from 240+ ratings centers on two themes: durability and accuracy. Multiple reviewers mention these are "the last measuring spoons you'll ever buy." One wrote, "I've gone through three cheap sets in the last five years. These are built like they'll outlive me."

Another recurring point: the spoons are flat enough to level off accurately with a knife, yet deep enough to hold wet ingredients. That's a genuinely rare combination. Most stamped spoons are either too shallow for liquids or too rounded to level dry ingredients properly.

## Where It Falls Short

Let's be honest about the flaws. The split ring design is functional but annoying — you have to pull the entire set apart to grab a single spoon. If you're mid-recipe with flour on your hands, that's a small frustration every single time.

The missing 1/8 teaspoon is a legitimate gap. If you bake frequently, you'll likely need that increment for recipes calling for 1/8 teaspoon of salt or baking soda. You can buy a single 1/8 teaspoon spoon separately, but that's an extra purchase and an extra item cluttering your drawer.

And yes, the price. These typically run $30–40, which is 3–4 times what a perfectly functional set costs. You're paying for the Le Creuset name and the build quality — both are real, but only you can decide if that premium is worth it.
