**Glass bread pans are making a comeback, and this EATEX set makes a strong case for why.**

You've probably baked bread in metal pans your whole life. Maybe you've got a scratched nonstick loaf pan that's seen better days. Here's the thing: glass changes the game in one specific way — you can *see* your bread browning. The EATEX set (two 9x5-inch pans, as shown in the product image) lets you watch the crust develop through the transparent walls. That's not a gimmick; that's a legitimately useful feature for anyone who's ever pulled a loaf five minutes too late.

**What you actually get**

The box contains two identical 9x5-inch glass loaf pans. They're made from borosilicate-style tempered glass, which handles oven temps up to 450°F. Each pan weighs about 2.5 pounds — noticeably heavier than aluminum, but that weight also means they won't warp or dent after years of use. They're dishwasher safe, though hand-washing is gentler on the glass.

**How they perform in real kitchens**

The tradeoff with glass is real: it conducts heat slower than metal. Your bread will need roughly 5–7 extra minutes in the oven compared to a metal pan recipe. The crust comes out slightly softer, more like a sandwich loaf than a crusty artisan boule. But here's what you gain: even browning on all sides, no hot spots, and a clear view of that golden-brown top before you commit to pulling the pan.

One reviewer noted: *"I could finally see when the sides were done without peeking and losing oven heat. My sandwich bread comes out perfect every time now."* Another mentioned using them for meatloaf: *"The glass releases the loaf cleanly after a 10-minute rest. No more stuck-on mess."*

**Where they fall short**

The thermal shock warning is worth repeating. If you pull a hot pan from a 400°F oven and immediately run cold water over it, you're asking for a crack. Let the pan cool for 10–15 minutes before washing. Also, if you're chasing a crusty, crackly exterior on your sourdough, glass isn't your best tool — metal or cast iron gives you that hard crust. And the weight is a genuine consideration for anyone with arthritis or grip issues.

**Who should buy these**

- **Bread beginners** — the see-through design removes the guesswork from "is it done?"
- **Meatloaf and pound cake bakers** — glass handles both sweet and savory with zero flavor retention
- **Anyone replacing warped metal pans** — these will outlast your oven

**Skip them if** you're a sourdough purist needing maximum crust crunch, or if you prefer the lighter weight of aluminum for frequent handling.

**FAQ**

**Can these go in the microwave or freezer?**
Yes. The glass is freezer-safe for dough storage and microwave-safe for reheating. Just avoid extreme temperature swings — don't go straight from freezer to a preheated oven.

**Do I need to grease glass pans differently?**
Yes. Glass needs a bit more lubrication than metal. Use butter, oil, or cooking spray generously, especially on the bottom corners. Some bakers swear by a parchment paper sling for extra insurance.

**Will my bread recipe need adjustments?**
Lower the oven temperature by 25°F from what your metal-pan recipe calls for, and add 5–7 minutes to the bake time. The glass heats more gently, so this keeps the outside from over-browning before the inside sets.

**The Verdict**

**Buy it if** you want foolproof visual feedback on your bakes, value durability over weight, and make sandwich bread, quick breads, or meatloaf regularly. **Skip it if** you need maximum crust crispness or can't manage the heavier weight.
