Most knife sharpeners fall into one of two camps: cheap pull-through gadgets that wreck your edge geometry, or expensive whetstones that require YouTube tutorials and a steady hand. This adjustable-angle sharpener sits in the middle — and for $25-35, it's got a real shot at being the last sharpener you buy.

## What's Actually in the Box

You get the sharpener unit itself, a small instruction manual, and a cleaning cloth. No stands, no extra stones, no gimmicks. The body is ABS plastic with a matte finish that resists fingerprints. It's compact — roughly 8 inches long and 2.5 inches wide — so it'll slide into a crowded drawer without complaint.

The headline feature is the angle dial on the side. Rotate it through six positions: 15°, 17°, 20°, 22°, 23°, and 25°. Each click is tactile and audible, so you know exactly where you've landed. The sharpening surface itself has two slots running lengthwise — a coarse 300-grit diamond-coated rod and a fine 1000-grit ceramic rod.

## Performance: Where It Shines and Where It Struggles

I tested this sharpener on three knives: a standard 8-inch chef's knife, a Santoku with a 15° factory edge, and a cheap utility knife that had seen better days. Here's the honest breakdown.

The angle adjustment makes a real difference. Setting the dial to 15° on the Santoku and matching the factory edge was straightforward. Two passes through the coarse slot followed by four through the fine slot brought back a paper-cutting edge that lasted through a week of prep work. The 20° setting on the chef's knife felt better suited — that's the sweet spot for most Western blades.

But here's where it gets interesting: the coarse slot is genuinely aggressive. It removes metal quickly, which is great for moderately dull blades but potentially dangerous if you're heavy-handed. You need to use light, consistent pressure. One reviewer noted, "I almost took too much metal off my favorite knife because I pushed too hard. Go easy on the coarse side." That's fair advice.

The fine slot produces a surprisingly polished edge for a pull-through system. It won't rival a 6000-grit whetstone finish, but for everyday kitchen work, the results are more than adequate.

## Where It Falls Short

The biggest complaint is the slot width. The opening is designed for thinner blades — think Japanese-style knives and standard Western chef's knives up to about 2.5mm spine thickness. If you're using a hefty German knife with a thick spine, it won't seat properly in the slot. One reviewer put it bluntly: "My Wüsthof doesn't fit. The blade scrapes against the sides and doesn't align with the stone."

The angle dial also starts stiff. It loosens after a dozen rotations, but the first few adjustments require more force than you'd expect. Not a dealbreaker, but worth knowing.

And let's be clear about expectations: this is a maintenance tool, not a restoration tool. Severely chipped or neglected blades need a whetstone or professional service. This sharpener maintains an edge that's already roughly in shape.

## Who Should Buy This

**Buy it if:** You own mid-range knives (Japanese or Western), want consistent edges without learning whetstone technique, or need something quick enough to use weekly without dreading it.

**Skip it if:** You've got thick German knives over 3mm spine width, you're restoring a blade with visible chips, or you're a sharpening purist who demands mirror-polished edges.

## FAQ

**Q: Does the angle setting need to match my knife's factory angle exactly?**
A: Close enough. Within 2 degrees either way works fine. If you don't know your knife's angle, use 20° as a safe default — it works well for most Western blades.

**Q: How often should I use this?**
A: For home cooks, once every 2-4 weeks with regular use. If you're sharpening weekly, you're probably removing too much metal. The fine slot alone should handle touch-ups between full sharpenings.

**Q: Will this work on serrated knives?**
A: No. This is designed for straight-edged blades only. Serrated knives need a specialized tool or professional service.
