## What you're actually getting

Open the box and you'll find the Hub unit itself, one filter cartridge, and a drip tray. That's it. No special faucet adapter or mounting hardware — the Hub runs on electricity and pulls water from its own 1.5-liter reservoir that you fill manually. That's the key design difference from under-sink systems: you're not plumbing this in. You fill the tank, the filter purifies on demand, and a small pump pushes water out through the dispense spout.

The unit measures roughly 11.8 inches wide and 14 inches tall. It's noticeably more compact than the older Brita Stream or a full-sized water cooler, which makes it a realistic option for cramped rental kitchens or dorm rooms.

---

## How it actually performs

The filtration story is legitimate. Brita says the Hub's carbon block filter reduces over 70 contaminants — lead, mercury, chlorine, PFAS, and microplastics among them. Independent lab certifications back the claims, which matters if you're replacing a basic pitcher filter. The digital display shows filter life as a percentage, so there's no guessing when to swap cartridges.

Dispensing is where the Hub splits opinion. The 1:1 water-to-filter ratio means you're not chasing a half-empty pitcher refill — but it also means you're waiting roughly 30 seconds for a 16-ounce glass. That's fine for filling a water bottle while you pack lunch. It's annoying when you need four glasses for dinner. The pump is audible but not loud — think soft whir, not blender.

One thing the product images don't show clearly: you must refill the reservoir frequently. The 1.5-liter tank holds about six standard glasses. For a family of four, that's three or four refills a day. It's not hard — just lift the top cover and pour — but it's a daily habit, not a set-and-forget system.

## Where it falls short

The filter cost is the biggest gripe. At roughly $50 per cartridge and a 3-month lifespan, that's $200 a year — more than triple the cost of Brita's pitcher filters. Do the math before you buy. If you're a single person drinking 4 glasses a day, a $30 pitcher with $12 filters might serve you better.

The short power cord is a genuine design flaw. The Hub needs to sit within about 2.5 feet of an outlet. In many kitchens, that's fine — but if your outlets are behind appliances, you'll need an extension cord, which looks clunky on a countertop.

Also worth noting: the drip tray is small. If you're filling a tall bottle, you'll want to remove it. And there's no temperature control — this dispenses room-temperature water only. If you want cold water, you're still keeping a pitcher in the fridge.

## Who should buy this

**Buy it if:** You drink 6+ glasses of water daily, hate pitcher refills, and value instant filtration over cost. It's also a strong pick for renters who can't install under-sink systems or for small households with one person who drinks the bulk of the water.

**Skip it if:** You're on a tight budget, want cold water on demand, or need to fill multiple glasses at once for a family. In those cases, a traditional pitcher or a countertop cooler with a cold tank makes more sense.

## FAQ

**Q: How often do I really need to replace the filter?**
Every 3 months or about 100 gallons, whichever comes first. The digital indicator tracks usage, so you'll see a percentage countdown. If you use less water, the indicator will stretch the timeline.

**Q: Does it remove fluoride?**
No. The carbon block filter targets chlorine, lead, PFAS, and microplastics but does not remove fluoride. If that's a priority, you need reverse osmosis.

**Q: Can I use it without electricity?**
No. The pump requires power. If you're preparing for emergencies or off-grid use, this isn't the right product.
