## What's actually in the box

The plastic is PET (polyethylene terephthalate), the same material as water bottles. It's crystal clear, not that hazy polypropylene you see in cheap party cups. As shown in the product image, they stack cleanly without sticking together, which saves you the annoying "peel apart 50 cups" dance at the start of a party.

## Real-world performance

I tested these the way actual buyers use them: ice water, iced coffee, lemonade with pulp, and a few aggressive shakes. The 16 oz cup held a full cocktail shaker's worth of ice and liquid with zero flex. If you're used to standard 12 oz diner cups, these look more like a 10 oz. The diameter is wide, so liquid volume is correct, but the proportions feel squat.

## Where they fall short

Here's the honest downside. These are PET cups — they are **not** safe for hot beverages. If you're thinking about using them for coffee or tea, stop. The listing is clear about cold use, but some buyers missed that and got burn-your-hand cups. That's a user error, but the listing could make it more obvious.

Second, there are no measurement lines. If you're making batch cocktails or portioning drinks for a crowd, you'll be eyeballing it. For a product marketed partially at caterers, that's a miss.

Third, the 12 oz size is visually misleading. Several reviews noted they look smaller than expected in photos, and I agree. If you're buying for portion control, fine. If you need cups that feel substantial, stick to the 16 oz.
