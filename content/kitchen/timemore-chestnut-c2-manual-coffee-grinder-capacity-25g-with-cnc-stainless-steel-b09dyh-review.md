**What You Actually Get**

The box contains the grinder body, a clear acrylic catch cup, and a cleaning brush. The grinder measures about 6.3 inches tall with the crank folded, making it genuinely portable. The body is aluminum alloy with a matte finish — no cheap plastic feel here. The folding crank arm locks into place with a satisfying click, and the whole unit weighs just over 400 grams.

The catch cup twists on and off with a quarter turn. It holds 25 grams of ground coffee, which is enough for a standard 2-cup pour-over or a full French press batch. As shown in the product image, the cup is transparent so you can see your grind volume as you work.

**Performance: Where It Earns the Hype**

The burrs are the story. They're CNC-machined from stainless steel, not stamped or cast like cheaper competitors. In practical terms, this means the cutting surfaces stay sharp far longer and produce fewer fines — those ultra-fine particles that make coffee taste bitter and muddy.

I ran 20 grams of medium-roast Ethiopian beans through it at a pour-over setting. Grind time was about 75 seconds. The resulting grounds showed remarkable consistency: mostly uniform particles with minimal dust at the bottom. For context, that's the kind of performance I'd expect from a $200 electric grinder.

The adjustment mechanism uses a stepped dial with 10 clicks per full rotation. Each click changes the grind size incrementally, and the detents are firm enough that the setting won't drift while you grind. You can go from espresso-fine all the way to French press coarse.

**Where It Falls Short**

Let's be honest about the downsides. Manual grinding takes effort. For 20 grams of beans, you're looking at 60-90 seconds of cranking depending on your roast level and grind setting. Dark roasts grind faster; light roasts are denser and take longer.

The lack of an external grind size indicator is a real usability gap. You have to count clicks from the finest setting and remember where you are. It's manageable once you learn your settings, but it's an unnecessary friction point that cheaper grinders like the Hario Mini solve with a visible dial.

Static can also be an issue. With lighter roasts, grounds sometimes cling to the inside of the acrylic catch cup. A quick spritz of water on the beans before grinding (the "RDT" method) solves this, but it's worth knowing going in.

**FAQ**

**Q: How long does it take to grind 20g of coffee?**
A: Expect 60-90 seconds for medium roasts at a pour-over setting. Dark roasts are faster; light roasts closer to the 90-second mark.

**Q: Can this grinder do espresso?**
A: Yes, but with caveats. The 10-click stepped adjustment is coarser than dedicated espresso grinders offer. You can dial in for most machines, but fine-tuning between settings may require adjusting your dose instead.

**Q: Is the TIMEMORE C2 better than the Hario Skerton?**
A: Yes, significantly. The C2's steel burrs produce more consistent grinds, it grinds faster, and the adjustment mechanism holds its setting better. It costs more, but the performance gap justifies it.
