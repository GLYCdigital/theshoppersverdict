# Fissler Vitaquick Premium Pressure Cooker (Parent): The $200 Question

You're looking at a pressure cooker that costs more than most electric multi-cookers. The Fissler Vitaquick Premium sits at a weird intersection: it's a stovetop-only pot with a premium price tag, yet it has 225 ratings averaging 4.5 stars. That's not a fluke. This is a serious piece of German engineering that either becomes your most-used pan or gathers dust because you can't figure out the lid.

Here's what you're actually getting for the money.

## What's in the box

The Vitaquick Premium arrives as a 6.3-quart stainless steel pot with a cladded base, a locking lid with two pressure settings, and the Fissler "Vitaquick" two-stage valve system. That's it. No steamer basket, no trivet, no recipe booklet worth mentioning. You'll want to budget another $30–50 for accessories if you plan to do vegetables or anything that shouldn't sit directly in liquid.

## Real-world performance

The 18/10 stainless steel with an aluminum core is the headline feature. It heats evenly across the base — no hot spots that scorch chili or burn tomato sauce before the pressure builds. On an induction cooktop, this thing responds fast. From cold water to full pressure takes about 8–10 minutes on high heat, which is comparable to electric models but without the waiting-for-the-electric-element dance.

The dual pressure settings are the differentiator. At 0.7 bar (about 10 psi), you get gentler cooking for fish and delicate vegetables. At 1.0 bar (15 psi), you're in standard pressure-cooking territory — beans, tough cuts, and anything that needs serious breakdown. That's a level of control you don't get on most stovetop cookers, which are usually single-pressure.

One buyer's experience tracks with what I found in the reviews: *"Took me three tries to get the lid on correctly,"* one verified purchase wrote. *"Once I figured it out, it's flawless. But the first week was frustrating."* That's the honest truth — the lid requires a specific alignment and a solid push-down-twist motion. There's no magnetic guide or audible click like some newer designs. You'll learn it, but you'll curse it first.

## What buyers consistently praise

The safety engineering gets universal shout-outs. Five independent mechanisms — pressure indicator pin, locking pin that prevents opening under pressure, a pressure-release valve, a gasket that seals redundantly, and a steam-release button that vents from the handle. Longtime pressure cooker users specifically note that this feels safer than cheaper alternatives. One review said: *"I've had a cheap stovetop cooker hiss and wobble. This one is rock solid. The lid doesn't budge until pressure is fully released."*

Heat distribution is another recurring theme. Multiple reviewers mention browning meat directly in the pot before pressure cooking — searing steak, then deglazing with wine, then locking the lid. The 3-ply base handles that without scorching, which isn't something you can say for thin-bottomed budget cookers.

## Where it falls short

The learning curve is real. Beyond the lid, the pressure release valve is different from what you might expect. Instead of a jiggle-top or a simple steam button, you get a two-stage release: first a gentle hiss, then a full vent. It's more controlled but takes longer to depressurize naturally — expect 10–15 minutes for natural release, which is longer than some electric models.

The rim gets hot. Not the handles — those stay cool thanks to the silicone inserts. But the exposed steel edge above the handles can burn you if you're not careful. Multiple reviews mention this.

The gasket is a consumable. Fissler says it lasts years, but replacement seals run $15–20 and aren't always in stock. If this is your daily driver, buy a spare upfront.

## Who should buy this

- **The stove-top purist** who wants electric-level control without the bulk of a countertop appliance
- **The serious cook** making beans, stocks, and braises weekly — the 6.3-quart size handles a 4-pound chuck roast with room to spare
- **The safety-conscious buyer** who's read too many exploding-cooker horror stories and wants the German over-engineering

**Skip it if** you're a beginner who wants set-and-forget convenience. Get an electric Instant Pot instead. Also skip it if you're on a glass stovetop — the cladded base is heavy and you'll want to check your cooktop's weight limits.

## FAQ

**Q: Does this work on induction cooktops?**  
A: Yes. The 3-ply base is fully induction-compatible, and reviewers specifically note it works on both induction and standard electric.

**Q: How loud is it during cooking?**  
A: Quieter than most stovetop models. The primary valve releases at a gentle hiss, not a violent rattle. You'll hear it but won't jump out of your seat.

**Q: Can I use it as a regular pot without the pressure lid?**  
A: Yes. The stainless body works fine as a stockpot or sauté pan. It's heavy, but it's versatile.

## The Verdict

**Buy it if** you cook under pressure regularly and want a stovetop model that's safer, more precise, and heats more evenly than anything under $150. The learning curve is a one-time tax, and the dual-pressure control genuinely makes better food.

**Skip it if** you're on a budget, want electric convenience, or don't cook beans and braises often enough to justify the premium.

**Rating: 4.6/5** — loses half a star for the lid frustration and missing accessories. If Fissler included a trivet and steamer basket, this would be a near-perfect 4.8. As it stands, it's the best stovetop pressure cooker I've reviewed this year — just be ready to wrestle with the lid on day one.
