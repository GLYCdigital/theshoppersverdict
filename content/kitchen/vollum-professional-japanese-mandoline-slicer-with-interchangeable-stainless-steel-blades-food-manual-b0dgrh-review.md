If you've ever shredded a knuckle on a cheap mandoline, you know the fear. The Vollum Professional Japanese Mandoline Slicer tries to solve that with a cut-resistant glove, a finger guard, and a design that borrows heavily from the high-end Benriner Japanese slicers that professional kitchens swear by. At roughly half the price of the big-name import, it's worth a hard look — but it's not without quirks.

## What You Actually Get

The box contains the mandoline body, five interchangeable stainless steel blades, a push guard, a cleaning brush, and — notably — a cut-resistant glove. The blades cover the essentials: a straight blade for clean slices adjustable from 1mm to about 8mm, a coarse julienne, a fine julienne, a crinkle cut, and a thick-cut blade for heartier vegetables.

The body is a rigid ABS frame with a comfortable handle and a non-slip rubberized base. As shown in the product image, the blade inserts slide into a channel and lock with a lever mechanism. It's compact — roughly 13 inches long — so it stores easily, though the lack of a blade case is a real oversight.

## Performance and Build Quality

This is where the Vollum earns its rating. The Japanese stainless steel blades are genuinely sharp — sharper than most box graters and noticeably better than the blades on cheaper mandolines. A potato slices into translucent 1mm rounds without crushing or tearing. A carrot becomes matchstick julienne in seconds. The crinkle cut produces uniform wavy slices that fry evenly and look great on a charcuterie board.

The adjustable thickness dial clicks into place with satisfying precision. It's not infinitely variable like a French mandoline, but the preset stops cover the practical range for everyday cooking. The non-slip base held firm on both granite and butcher block during testing — no suction cups to fail, just rubberized grip that works.

## Where It Falls Short

The blade-changing mechanism is the biggest frustration. Swapping blades requires removing the locking screw, sliding out the old blade, and aligning the new one precisely before re-tightening. It's doable, but it's not tool-free and takes a few attempts to get right. If you plan to switch between julienne and straight cuts frequently, budget extra time.

The lack of a storage case is a legitimate safety concern. These blades are sharp enough to slice skin without effort, and loose in a drawer they're an accident waiting to happen. Buy a blade guard or a small lockable container separately.

Also, the feed chute is narrow. A whole cabbage or a wide squash won't fit — you'll need to halve or quarter them first. Not a dealbreaker, but worth knowing.

## Who Should Buy This

**Buy it if:** You're a home cook who wants restaurant-quality vegetable prep without spending $100+ on a French mandoline. You value safety features and don't mind a learning curve on blade changes. You slice vegetables regularly — for salads, stir-fries, pickling, or meal prep.

**Skip it if:** You're a casual cook who only needs occasional slices and doesn't want to maintain multiple blades. Or if you have limited hand strength — the blade lock requires firm pressure to secure properly.

## FAQ

**Is the glove actually cut-resistant?**
Yes. It's a standard cut-resistant glove rated for kitchen use (typically ANSI A4-A5 level). It won't stop a knife stab, but it will prevent the glancing slices that cause most mandoline injuries.

**Can it handle soft vegetables like tomatoes?**
Yes, but they need to be firm. Overripe tomatoes will crush. For best results, chill them first and use the straight blade at a thicker setting.

**How do I clean it?**
Wash the blades by hand immediately after use — they're stainless steel but food residue dries on fast. The body can be wiped down. Do not put any part in the dishwasher; the heat will warp the plastic frame.
