**The Short Version:** If you've played the original Ticket to Ride and wondered when your kid can join in, First Journey is the answer — but it's a stepping stone, not a destination.

---

**What's Actually in the Box**

You get the game board (a simplified US map with bold, color-coded routes), 72 colored train cars, 32 destination tickets, 4 scoring markers, and a deck of colored train cards. The components match the adult version's quality — thick cardboard, vivid colors, and trains that feel substantial. The box itself is smaller than the original, which makes it easier to store and haul to grandma's house.

**How It Plays (and Why It Works)**

The rules are stripped down to the essentials: collect colored train cards, claim routes between cities, and complete your destination tickets. The twist that makes this kid-friendly — you only need to collect 6 train cards to win, not build the longest route or chase points. First player to finish their tickets and claim 6 routes wins.

What impressed me most is how the design preserves the original's decision-making. Your kid still has to choose between hoarding cards for a long route or grabbing short ones to finish faster. That tension — the heart of Ticket to Ride — survives the simplification. The board uses larger, clearer icons and the routes are color-matched to make matching cards to tracks nearly foolproof.

**What Buyers Consistently Praise**

The 4.8-star rating (240+ reviews) isn't an accident. Parents repeatedly highlight two things:

- **The "just right" difficulty.** One reviewer wrote: *"My 6-year-old picked it up in one sitting, but my 8-year-old still finds it challenging because everyone plays differently."* That's the sweet spot — simple enough to learn fast, strategic enough to stay interesting.
- **It fixes the original's biggest flaw for families.** The original Ticket to Ride drags on for 60-90 minutes. First Journey wraps in under 30. As another parent put it: *"We can play twice before bedtime, and my kids actually want to play again."*

**Where It Falls Short**

The fixed map is the biggest limitation. Once your family learns the optimal routes on the US map, the replay value drops. There are no expansion maps for this version, so you're stuck with the same geography until your kid outgrows it. That's fine for a $20-30 game, but families who play weekly will hit the ceiling in a few months.

Second, the scoring is binary — you either finish your tickets or you don't. That removes the "longest route" bonus and point-chasing that gives the adult version its depth. Kids who've played other strategy games may find it too simple by age 9 or 10.

**Who Should Buy This**

- **Parents of kids 6-8** who want a genuine strategy game, not just luck-based rolls
- **Families who already own Ticket to Ride** and want a version the youngest can play without help
- **Grandparents or gift-givers** looking for a screen-free activity that actually engages kids

Skip it if your kid is 10+ and already comfortable with games like Catan Junior or Kingdomino — they'll outgrow this in a few sessions.

**FAQ: What Shoppers Actually Ask**

**Q: Can a 5-year-old play this?**
The box says 6+, and that's accurate. A 5-year-old could play with heavy parent guidance, but they'll struggle with the strategic choices. Wait until 6.

**Q: Does it teach anything?**
Geography, mostly — kids learn city locations and US regions. The card-matching reinforces color recognition and simple planning.

**Q: Is it compatible with the original Ticket to Ride?**
No. The components and board are not interchangeable with the adult version.

**The Verdict**

**Buy it if** you want a legitimate strategy game for a 6-8 year old that respects their intelligence without frustrating them. **Skip it if** you need something that'll grow with your kid beyond age 10, or if you want a game with high replay variety.

At 4.6/5, this is the best entry-level strategy game for young kids I've tested. It's not perfect — the fixed map limits long-term replay — but for what it does, it does exceptionally well. Your kid will learn real strategy, and you'll enjoy playing with them. That's worth the price of admission.
