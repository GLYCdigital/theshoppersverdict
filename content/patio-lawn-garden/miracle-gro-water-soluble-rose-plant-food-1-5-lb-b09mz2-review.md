**What’s actually in the box**  
You get one 1.5 lb cardboard box of fine, blue-green powder. That’s it. No scoop, no sprayer. The label says 1.5 lb feeds 24 gallons of solution — that’s roughly 12–14 full watering cans or about 8–10 weeks of weekly feeding for a small rose bed. The NPK ratio is 18-24-16, which is heavy on phosphorus (the middle number) — exactly what roses need to push out buds instead of just leaves.  

**Real-world performance**  
I mixed a tablespoon into a gallon of water and drenched the base of three hybrid tea roses in early June. Within eight days, new growth was visibly darker green, and buds fattened noticeably. By day 12, I had blooms that were a full centimeter larger in diameter than the previous flush. The phosphorus boost is real — it’s not placebo gardening.  

The powder dissolves completely in lukewarm water. No gritty sediment at the bottom of the can, no clogged sprayer nozzles. You can use it on container roses too, but cut the dose in half — pots don’t have the soil buffer that garden beds do.

**Where it falls short**

Second, the lack of a measuring scoop is annoying — you’ll use a kitchen teaspoon, which is fine until you accidentally grab the tablespoon and burn your plants. Third, it’s not organic. If you’re strictly chemical-free, this isn’t your product. One reviewer warned: *“Great stuff, but I overdid it once and got brown leaf edges. Follow the label exactly.”* That’s accurate — nitrogen burn is real if you eyeball the dose.

**FAQ from real shoppers**  
**Q: Can I use this on other flowers?**  
Yes. It’s formulated for roses, but it works on dahlias, hibiscus, and annuals. Just dilute it to half strength for smaller plants.  

**Q: How often should I feed during summer heat?**  
Stick to every 7 days, but skip feeding when temps exceed 95°F — the plants are stressed and won’t absorb it well. Resume when it cools.  

**Q: Does it work in a hose-end sprayer?**  
Yes, but mix the powder in a small bucket of warm water first to fully dissolve, then pour into the sprayer reservoir. Dry powder clumps in the hopper.
