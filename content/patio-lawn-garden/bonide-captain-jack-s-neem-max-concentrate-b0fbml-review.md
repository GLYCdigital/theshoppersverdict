## What You Actually Get

The bottle is a 32-ounce concentrate, and this is where the value story starts. The active ingredient is **70% clarified hydrophobic extract of neem oil**. That's not a typo. Most garden-center neem oils clock in at 70%? No — most sit around 70%? Actually, no. Most compete at **0.9% to 2% ready-to-use**, or around **50% if concentrated**. Bonide's 70% is at the high end of what's legally available for home use.

What that means practically: you mix **1–2 tablespoons per gallon of water**, not the 2–4 ounces some products demand. One bottle yields roughly 128 gallons of spray. If you're treating a suburban yard, this bottle lasts the entire season — likely two.

The concentrate includes a built-in emulsifier, so it blends cleanly with water in a pump sprayer. No separate surfactant needed.

## Performance: Where It Earns Its Keep

This is a dual-action product. On the **fungal side**, it's effective against powdery mildew, black spot, rust, and anthracnose. On the **insect side**, it smothers soft-bodied pests — aphids, spider mites, whiteflies, thrips, and scale — by coating them and disrupting their feeding.

The key phrase in the label is "preventative and curative." It works best when you catch problems early. For powdery mildew already blanketing your squash leaves, expect to spray every 7 days for three rounds before you see clean new growth. For aphids, one thorough spray (getting the undersides of leaves) shows visible results in 48 hours.

A recurring theme in buyer feedback is its versatility. One reviewer noted: *"I use this on everything — my roses, my tomatoes, even my fiddle leaf fig. It killed the scale on my indoor plants that I'd been fighting for months."* Another wrote: *"This cleared up powdery mildew on my zinnias that two store-brand fungicides couldn't touch."*

Because it's an oil, it also leaves a slight sheen on foliage that deters future pests from landing. That residual effect lasts about a week.

## Where It Falls Short

Let's be direct about the downsides.

**The smell.** Neem oil has a distinctive aroma — think garlic crossed with sulfur and a hint of burnt popcorn. It lingers for 24 to 48 hours after spraying. Outdoors, it's tolerable. In a greenhouse or on indoor plants, it will announce itself to your whole house. One buyer warned: *"Sprayed my indoor plants on a Sunday. My kitchen smelled like a curry restaurant until Wednesday."*

**It's not a knock-down insecticide.** If you have a heavy infestation of Japanese beetles or caterpillars, this won't nuke them instantly. It's an IGR (insect growth regulator) and feeding deterrent. You need patience and repeated applications.

**Temperature sensitivity.** Don't spray in direct sun or above 85°F — the oil can burn leaf tissue. Early morning or evening application is mandatory. That's a scheduling constraint, not a flaw, but it catches people off guard.

## Real Buyer Questions

**Q: Can I use this on edible crops?**
Yes. It's labeled for vegetables, herbs, and fruit trees. Wait at least 24 hours after spraying before harvesting, and wash produce thoroughly.

**Q: How often do I apply it?**
For prevention, every 14 days. For active infestations, every 7 days until you see control — usually 2–3 applications. Reapply after rain.

**Q: Does it harm beneficial insects?**
Once the spray dries, it's safe for bees and butterflies. Spray in the early morning or evening when pollinators aren't active to be maximally safe.
