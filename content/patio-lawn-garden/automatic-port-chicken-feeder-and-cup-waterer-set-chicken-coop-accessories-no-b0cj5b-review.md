## Does This Auto-Fill Set Actually Save You Time?

If you're tired of refilling poultry waterers twice a day or watching chickens scratch feed onto the ground, the Automatic Port Chicken Feeder and Cup Waterer Set promises a fix. It's a two-piece system designed to work with standard 5-gallon buckets, turning them into gravity-fed stations. At $30–$40 depending on the listing, it's cheaper than buying a pre-made all-in-one unit. But does it hold up to real coop life? Let's break it down.

## What's Actually in the Box

The set includes two main components: a feeder base with a port opening and a cup-style waterer. The feeder base has a wide tray that screws onto the bottom of a bucket, with a hole that lets feed flow down as chickens eat from the ports. The waterer cup works similarly—it attaches to a bucket lid or bottom and fills a small drinking cup via suction or gravity, depending on how you mount it.

You'll also get mounting hardware (screws and grommets) to attach the waterer cup to a bucket. You provide the buckets—one for feed, one for water. That's a standard 5-gallon size, which is easy to source. Note that the water cup has a small float valve, not a nipple system. That matters for how often you clean it.

## Build Quality and Installation

The plastic is thick and UV-stabilized, which is good because it'll sit in direct sun. The feeder tray is sturdy and has a lip that does a decent job of catching stray feed. I've seen cheaper trays flex under a full bucket; this one holds its shape. The cup waterer's float mechanism is simple—no springs or complex parts to fail, just a floating disc that seals when the cup is full.

Installation takes about 15 minutes. The feeder screws onto the bucket threads cleanly. The water cup requires drilling a 3/4-inch hole in the bucket, inserting the grommet, and snapping the cup in place. If you're not handy with a drill, that step might frustrate you. The instructions are clear, but they assume basic tool skills.

## Real-World Performance

**The feeder works as advertised.** Chickens peck at the ports, feed drops down, and the tray refills automatically. I ran a two-week test with six hens, and feed waste dropped noticeably—maybe 20% less on the ground compared to an open trough. The ports are sized for standard hens; if you have bantams, they'll get in fine. If you have ducks, the ports are too small, and that's a dealbreaker for waterfowl owners.

**The water cup is where opinions split.** In my test, it kept water clean for about three days before needing a rinse. Algae grows in the cup because it's open and exposed to sunlight. That's not a flaw—all open cups do this—but it means you're still cleaning it weekly. One reviewer put it plainly: "The cup waterer is convenient, but I still scrub it every few days. Not a set-and-forget solution." Another praised it: "No more dumping slimy water every afternoon. My girls finally have clean water when I'm at work."

## Where It Falls Short

The float valve in the water cup is sensitive to debris. If you use unfiltered well water, sediment can jam the seal, causing the cup to overflow. You'll need to add a filter or use clean tap water. That's a real consideration for rural properties.

The feeder tray is shallow. If you have aggressive breeds like Orpingtons or Brahmas that push hard while eating, they can tip the tray off the bucket base. It snaps back on easily, but you'll find feed scattered occasionally. It's not a fatal flaw—just a quirk to know about.

Finally, the "automatic" claim is misleading for the waterer. The cup holds about 12 ounces, which is fine for a few hens but not a big flock. With more than eight birds, you'll still refill the bucket daily. This is a small-flock solution, not a commercial-grade setup.

## Who Should Buy This

- **Backyard chicken keepers with 3–8 hens** who want to cut down on daily feeding and watering trips.
- **People with standard-sized chickens** (not ducks, not bantams with tiny beaks—though bantams can manage).
- **Owners who already have 5-gallon buckets** and want an upgrade without buying a whole new unit.
- **Anyone who hates cleaning open trough waterers** and is okay with a weekly cup scrub instead.

Skip it if you have a large flock, use well water without filtration, or want a truly hands-off water system—look at nipple drinkers for that.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I use this with a metal bucket?**
A: No. The feeder screws onto the bucket threads, and metal buckets rarely have compatible threading. Stick with plastic 5-gallon buckets from hardware stores.

**Q: Will the waterer work in freezing temperatures?**
A: Not without a heater. The float valve will freeze solid in winter. You'll need a heated bucket base or switch to a different system during cold months.

**Q: How often do I need to clean the feeder?**
A: Every two weeks, dump out any stale feed and wipe the tray. The port design keeps most debris out, but fine dust from feed can accumulate and clog the flow if left too long.

## The Verdict

**Buy it if** you have a small-to-mid flock and want to cut daily chore time without spending $80+ on a pre-assembled unit. The feeder alone is worth the price—it genuinely reduces waste and keeps feed dry.

**Skip it if** you need a winter-proof water solution, have ducks, or expect zero maintenance. The cup waterer is convenient, not magical. It still needs weekly attention, and the float valve demands clean water to work reliably.

At 4.2 stars from 210 ratings, this matches my experience: a solid, well-priced feeder with a slightly overhyped waterer. It earns a 4.2/5 from me.
