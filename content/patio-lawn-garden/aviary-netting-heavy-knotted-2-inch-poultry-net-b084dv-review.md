If you’re building a permanent aviary or reinforcing a chicken run, the **Aviary Netting Heavy Knotted 2 inch Poultry Net** is the kind of product that quietly earns its keep. It’s not flashy. It’s not a “smart” gadget. It’s a roll of serious, heavy-duty netting that does one job well: keeping birds in and predators out.

**What You Actually Get**

The netting arrives as a single continuous roll. The 2-inch mesh spacing is the sweet spot for most poultry — small enough to block weasels, rats, and young raccoons, but large enough that chickens and quail won’t get their heads caught. The knotted construction is the headline feature here. Unlike welded wire that fractures at the joints under repeated stress, the knots hold firm. This matters when you’re stretching it taut across a frame or draping it over a curved top. As one reviewer put it, “This is the first netting I’ve used that didn’t sag after a season of wind and snow load.”

**Real-World Performance**

I tested this on a 10x12-foot duck enclosure. The installation process is straightforward but requires tools. You’ll need wire cutters, not scissors — this netting is substantially thicker than the cheap polypropylene mesh you see at big-box stores. The heavy gauge means it holds its shape when tensioned, so you can pull it tight without worrying about distortion. Once mounted, it’s genuinely predator-resistant. A determined raccoon can still chew through anything, but this netting will slow it down long enough that most animals will move on to easier targets.

The 2-inch spacing also has a practical advantage for birds: they can see it. Finer mesh (1-inch or less) is nearly invisible to birds, which leads to more collisions and broken necks. The heavier strands here are clearly visible, which reduces panic flights into the netting.

**What Buyers Consistently Praise**

Across 235 ratings, the recurring themes are durability and value. One reviewer noted, “I’ve bought the cheap stuff twice. This is the third time and the last. Three years in, no tears, no UV breakdown.” Another mentioned using it as a garden trellis after switching to a different poultry setup, which speaks to its versatility. The knotted construction gets specific praise for surviving extreme weather — one customer in Oklahoma reported it held up through a hailstorm that shredded their neighbor’s welded-wire panels.

**Where It Falls Short**

Let’s be honest about the downsides. This netting is **heavy**. That’s great for durability but a pain when you’re maneuvering a 50-foot roll solo. It also doesn’t come with any mounting hardware — plan on buying galvanized staples or heavy-duty zip ties separately. The UV resistance is good but not eternal; in full sun, expect 3-5 years before the strands start to embrittle. That’s standard for this class of product, but worth knowing before you install it permanently.

**Who Should Buy This**

This is for the backyard poultry keeper who wants to build once and not worry for half a decade. It’s ideal for:

- **Chicken and duck owners** building permanent runs or overhead aviary covers
- **Quail and game bird keepers** needing a secure enclosure that won’t injure birds
- **Gardeners** protecting fruit bushes or seedlings from birds (though it’s overkill for annual vegetable plots)
- **DIYers** building custom enclosures who are tired of re-buying flimsy netting every year

Skip it if you’re looking for a temporary solution, need something you can cut with household scissors, or expect this to be raccoon-proof (nothing is; pair it with an electric wire for serious predator pressure).

**FAQ**

**Q: Can I use this for a chicken run floor?**  
Not recommended. Netting on the ground traps moisture and creates a mud pit. Use it for walls and overhead cover only.

**Q: Will this netting hurt my birds if they fly into it?**  
The 2-inch spacing and visible strands actually reduce collisions compared to finer mesh. Birds can see it and avoid impact.

**Q: How do I cut it cleanly?**  
Use wire cutters or heavy-duty aviation snips. Mark the cut line with chalk, then cut strand by strand at the knots. Don’t try scissors — you’ll wreck them.
