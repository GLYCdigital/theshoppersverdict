Let’s be direct: You’re not buying this for a single flower or a striking centerpiece. You’re buying a workhorse. Creeping Jenny (Lysimachia nummularia) is the plant you toss into a hanging basket, a mixed container, or a bare patch of damp ground when you want fast, reliable, chartreuse coverage. At 4.1 stars from 204 ratings, most buyers agree it delivers — with a few caveats worth knowing before you add it to cart.

## What Actually Shows Up

You get a live plant in a standard 4-inch nursery pot, not a sad, dried-out cutting. The root system is established, which is the single biggest reason these tend to thrive rather than sulk. Expect several trailing stems, each 4–6 inches long, with the signature round, penny-sized leaves in bright lime green. The "Healthy" in the listing isn't just marketing — the packaging includes moisture retention and the plant arrives hydrated. If you're ordering in summer, unbox it the day it lands and pot it up immediately.

## Real-World Performance

This is a fast grower. In a hanging basket, expect it to spill over the edges within 3–4 weeks. As ground cover, it fills about 1–2 square feet per plant per season. The key? Consistent moisture. Creeping Jenny is not drought-tolerant — let it dry out completely and the leaves crisp at the edges and turn brown. Give it water, and it rewards you with that electric chartreuse color that pops against dark foliage or mulch.

Hardiness is a genuine strength. It survives winters down to Zone 3 and comes back reliably in spring. In warmer zones (8+), it can stay evergreen but will look ragged by late summer if exposed to intense afternoon sun. Morning sun with afternoon shade is the sweet spot.

## What Buyers Keep Saying

The consensus among the 204 ratings is remarkably consistent. One reviewer noted, *"Planted it in a large pot with a geranium and it completely took over the pot by August — exactly what I wanted."* Another praised the shipping: *"Came well-packed, no broken stems, and it's been growing like crazy for two months."* The most common complaints aren't about the plant itself — they're about shipping delays in extreme heat or the plant arriving slightly wilted (which recovers with water).

## Where It Falls Short

The honest truth: this plant is a thug. In garden beds, it spreads aggressively via rooting stems and can smother less vigorous neighbors. If you plant it in open ground, you will be pulling it out of places you didn't want it for years. It's not a problem if you're using it in containers or a contained border. It's a real problem if you're an orderly gardener who likes boundaries.

Also, the color fades to a dull green in deep shade. If you want that bright golden-lime look, you need at least 4–6 hours of sun.

## Who Should Buy This

- **Container gardeners** who want instant spill-over in baskets or mixed planters
- **Zone 3–7 homeowners** with a damp, shady spot that needs fast coverage
- **Anyone** who wants a low-maintenance (but not no-maintenance) trailing accent

**Skip it if** you have a manicured flower bed you don't want to police, or if you're in a hot, dry climate without irrigation.

## FAQ

**Q: Will it survive winter outdoors?**
A: Yes, down to Zone 3. It dies back to the roots and re-emerges in spring. In Zone 8+, it may stay semi-evergreen but looks best if cut back hard in late summer.

**Q: Can I plant it in full sun?**
A: Yes, but only with consistent moisture. If the soil dries out, the leaves will scorch. Morning sun with afternoon shade is safer in warmer zones.

**Q: How fast does it spread?**
A: In one season, a single plant covers roughly 1–2 square feet as ground cover. In a hanging basket, it trails 1–2 feet by fall.

## The Verdict

**Buy it if** you want a fast, forgiving, colorful filler for containers or a damp problem area, and you're willing to keep it watered and contained.

**Skip it if** you want a polite, slow-spreading plant or you garden in hot, dry conditions without regular watering.

**Rating: 4.2/5** — It does exactly what it promises, as long as you understand what you're planting. The invasive potential and water needs are the only real strikes against it. For container use, it's near-perfect.
