**Bottom line: This is the heavy-duty ice melter for people who are tired of re-salting their driveway three times per storm.** Peladow calcium chloride pellets aren't the cheapest option on the shelf, but they're the one that actually works when temperatures plunge below zero.

## What You're Actually Getting

The parent listing covers Peladow's calcium chloride pellets in white buckets—typically 50-pound containers, though sizes vary by seller. These aren't the blue-tinted rock salt crystals you're used to. They're dense, uniform white pellets roughly the size of small peas. That shape matters more than you'd think.

**The pellet form is the hidden winner here.** Rock salt flakes bounce everywhere when you spread them—half your bag ends up in the lawn or blown into a drift. These pellets drop where you aim them and stay put. One buyer noted, "I used half the product I normally use with rock salt because it wasn't bouncing into the grass."

## Performance: Does It Actually Melt Ice?

Yes, and it does so fast. Calcium chloride is exothermic—it generates heat as it dissolves. That means it starts chewing through ice immediately, even at 5 a.m. on a 10-degree morning. Rock salt needs the sun or vehicle traffic to activate; Peladow doesn't wait.

**Temperature range is the real differentiator.** Standard sodium chloride (rock salt) stops working effectively around 15°F. Peladow keeps melting down to -25°F. For anyone in the Upper Midwest, Northeast, or mountain states, that's the difference between a clear driveway and a skating rink for three months straight.

Buyers consistently praise the speed. One reviewer wrote, "This melts ice faster than anything I've tried. I put it down, went inside for coffee, and by the time I came out the ice was already breaking up."

## Where It Falls Short

**Concrete damage is a legitimate concern.** Calcium chloride can cause surface spalling (flaking) on concrete if you overapply or use it repeatedly. This isn't a Peladow-specific flaw—it's chemistry. But it means you can't just liberally broadcast it and walk away. Use a spreader at a moderate setting and don't pile it up.

**The slick-phase problem is real and rarely mentioned.** When pellets first hit ice, they dissolve into a liquid brine that's momentarily slippery. On a steep driveway, that brief window can be hazardous. Spread it, then wait—don't walk on it immediately.

**Pet owners need to know this isn't paw-friendly.** Calcium chloride is more irritating to dog paws than rock salt, and ingestion can cause gastrointestinal distress. If you have pets, you'll need booties or a pet-safe alternative for areas they frequent.

## Who Should Buy This

**Buy it if** you live in a region that sees single-digit temperatures, have a large driveway or walkway you need clear fast, and are willing to pay more per application for performance.

**Skip it if** you're in a mild-winter area that rarely drops below 20°F (rock salt is cheaper and sufficient), have pets that roam the treated areas, or have brand-new concrete you're worried about protecting.

## FAQ

**Q: Is one 50-pound bag enough for a standard two-car driveway?**
A: For an average storm, plan on roughly 1–2 pounds per 100 square feet. A 50-pound bag covers a standard two-car driveway (about 400–500 square feet) for 3–5 storms, depending on ice thickness and how heavy-handed you are.

**Q: Can I use this on my concrete steps or will it ruin them?**
A: You can, but you're trading longevity for convenience. Use it sparingly on steps, avoid piling it, and switch to sand or kitty litter for traction once the ice is broken up. The concrete damage risk comes from repeated overapplication, not occasional use.

**Q: How is this different from the blue pellets at the hardware store?**
A: Many blue pellets are a blend of sodium chloride and calcium chloride—you're paying for some calcium chloride content but still getting rock salt's limitations. Peladow is 94%+ pure calcium chloride, which is why it works at lower temperatures and melts faster.
