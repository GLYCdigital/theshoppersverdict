## Where It Falls Short

Let's be honest about the limitations. **The beeswax coating is not a permanent exterior finish.** In a dry climate, it'll last years. But if your hives sit in Pacific Northwest rain or Midwest snow, you'll want to apply an exterior-grade paint or oil over the wax on the exterior surfaces. The wax protects the wood, but it won't stand up to constant wet/dry cycling indefinitely. That's not a flaw — it's the nature of beeswax. Budget for it.

Also, **this is just the box.** No frames, no foundation, no bottom board. If you're starting from scratch, you'll spend another $25–35 per box outfitting it with frames and foundation. That's standard for brood boxes, but worth noting if you're pricing a full hive setup.

Finally, the routed handholds work fine for a deep brood box full of bees (roughly 40–50 lbs). But if you're planning to use these as honey supers, invest in a grip strap — the handholds get awkward with 80+ lbs of full honey frames.

## FAQ

**Q: Does the beeswax dip affect how bees use the box?**
A: Positively. Bees recognize the wax scent as familiar and often draw comb faster in wax-dipped boxes. The coating also seals the wood, preventing moisture absorption that leads to mold in the corners.

**Q: Is this box food-safe for honey production?**
A: Yes. 100% beeswax is naturally antimicrobial and food-safe. No chemicals, no solvent-based sealants. Beeswax is literally what bees produce — it's the most natural finish you can put on a hive.

**Q: Will the dovetail joints hold up to heavy use?**
A: In practice, yes. Dovetails are mechanically stronger than finger joints because the interlocking shape resists pulling apart. Multiple reviewers report prying frames free for years without any joint failure.
