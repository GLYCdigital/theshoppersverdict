**Opening: The 20,000-seed promise is real, but the path to a thyme lawn is paved with patience — and a few failures.**

If you're looking at this listing, you've probably seen those lush photos of creeping thyme spilling between pavers or replacing a water-guzzling lawn. The idea is beautiful. The execution? That's where things get complicated. With 165 ratings averaging 3.3 stars, this seed pack has a clear split personality: some buyers report a fragrant, flowering carpet within months, while others watched their seeds vanish into the soil without a trace.

**What's in the bag:** A single foil pouch containing 20,000+ tiny, dark brown seeds — roughly 0.02 grams each, which means you're getting about 0.4 ounces total. That's a legitimate volume for covering 500–1,000 square feet if you're using the recommended spacing of 4–6 inches between plants. The seeds are non-GMO and untreated, which matters if you're gardening organically or want to avoid chemical coatings. There's no starter tray, no soil, no instructions beyond what's printed on the label.

**Performance and build quality:** Creeping thyme (*Thymus serpyllum*) is a legitimate perennial ground cover — it's hardy in zones 4–9, tolerates light foot traffic, and releases that classic herbal aroma when you walk on it. Once established, it's genuinely low-maintenance and drought-tolerant. The problem is getting there.

Here's the honest reality from buyer feedback: germination is erratic. One reviewer noted, "I scattered these in spring, watered daily, and got maybe 30 sprouts out of the whole packet." Another countered, "Followed the soak-in-water trick and had hundreds of seedlings within three weeks." The difference? Cold stratification. Creeping thyme seeds often need 2–4 weeks of cold, moist conditions to break dormancy. If you're sowing directly outdoors in spring without a cold period, you're gambling.

The seeds are also minuscule — about the size of a grain of sand. Spreading them evenly by hand is nearly impossible. Multiple buyers recommend mixing the seeds with fine sand (a 1:4 ratio) before broadcasting. Without that, you'll get dense clumps in some spots and bare patches elsewhere.

**What buyers consistently praise:** When it works, it works beautifully. "Two years in and my stepping stones are framed with thick, fragrant thyme — bees love it," one verified purchase reported. The value proposition is also solid; 20,000 seeds at this price point is cheaper per square foot than buying nursery flats. For gardeners willing to stratify, start indoors, or sow in fall for natural winter chilling, this pack delivers.

**Where it falls short:** The biggest issue isn't the seeds themselves — it's the lack of guidance. No stratification instructions, no recommended planting depth (they need light to germinate, so no burying), no soil prep advice. For a product aimed at home gardeners, that's a significant omission. Some buyers also reported receiving seeds that were visibly mixed with chaff or plant debris, though this appears inconsistent batch-to-batch.

**Who should buy this:** 
- **Patient gardeners** willing to start seeds indoors or sow in fall for spring germination
- **Budget-conscious landscapers** covering large areas on a shoestring
- **Pollinator enthusiasts** who want a bee-friendly, low-mow alternative to grass

**Skip it if:** You want instant results, you're sowing in late spring/summer without refrigeration, or you need guaranteed high germination. This is a project seed, not a plug-and-play solution.

**FAQ:**

**Q: Do I really need to cold-stratify these seeds?**  
A: In most cases, yes. Mix the seeds with moist sand, seal in a bag, and refrigerate for 3–4 weeks before sowing. If you're planting in fall, the natural winter cold handles this for you.

**Q: How long until I have a full ground cover?**  
A: Realistically, 1–2 growing seasons. The first year is mostly sparse seedlings. By year two, established plants spread to form a mat. Anyone promising faster results is overselling.

**Q: Can I walk on creeping thyme?**  
A: Yes, but not constantly. Light foot traffic is fine; heavy daily use will bruise the foliage and inhibit growth. It's better for ornamental paths than a backyard soccer field.
