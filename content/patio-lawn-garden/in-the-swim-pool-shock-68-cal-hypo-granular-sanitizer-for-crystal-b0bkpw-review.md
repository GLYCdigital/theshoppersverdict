## Where it falls short

Here's the honest downside: cal-hypo adds calcium to your pool water. Every single pound of this shock contributes roughly 7–8 ppm of calcium hardness per 10,000 gallons. If you have a plaster or gunite pool, repeated use can eventually cause scaling on walls and tile. If your source water is already hard (over 300 ppm calcium), you'll need to monitor and occasionally use a calcium reducer or switch to a dichlor or lithium shock.

Saltwater pool owners should skip this entirely — the added calcium can interfere with salt chlorine generator performance over time.

Also note: this is a fast-acting oxidizer, not a stabilized chlorine. If your pool sits in direct sun all day, the chlorine will burn off quickly. You'll want to pair this with a cyanuric acid stabilizer or use it in the evening. That's not a flaw specific to this product — it's how cal-hypo works — but worth knowing before you buy.

## FAQ

**Q: How often should I shock with 68% cal-hypo?**
A: Weekly during swim season for regular maintenance, or after heavy use, rainstorms, or visible cloudiness. For algae blooms, double-dose and brush thoroughly, then re-test after 24 hours.

**Q: Can I use this in an above-ground pool with a vinyl liner?**
A: Yes — this is actually one of the safer options for vinyl because it dissolves cleanly without bleaching or staining, provided you pre-dissolve granules in a bucket of pool water before adding.

**Q: Does this affect pH levels?**
A: Cal-hypo has a near-neutral pH, so it won't swing your pH like liquid chlorine (sodium hypochlorite) does. You may still need minor pH adjustments, but it's gentler on your chemistry overall.
