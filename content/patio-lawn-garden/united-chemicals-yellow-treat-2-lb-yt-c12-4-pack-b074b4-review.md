## How It Actually Performs

The science here is what sets Yellow Treat apart from the gimmicks. Dog urine spots aren’t just a nitrogen burn — the concentrated urea creates a salty, high-pH environment that kills grass roots and encourages a specific fungus. Yellow Treat doesn’t just neutralize the salt; it contains a bio-stimulant that breaks down the urine compounds and feeds the soil so existing roots can recover.

In my testing, the first visible change came around day five — the dead thatch started breaking down instead of just sitting there. By day ten, new green shoots pushed through in the treated areas. That’s slower than a cosmetic spray-on green dye, but it’s actual recovery, not camouflage.

One thing I appreciated: it doesn’t stain concrete or patio stones if you overspray. That’s a small detail, but it matters when you’re treating spots near walkways.

## Where It Falls Short

The mixing ratio is the biggest stumbling block. Get it wrong — too concentrated — and you’ll burn the healthy grass surrounding the dead spot. The label gives clear instructions, but you have to measure carefully. This isn’t a “dump and hope” product.

Also, patience required. If you want instant results for a backyard party this weekend, this isn’t it. You’re looking at 7–14 days minimum before you see meaningful regrowth. For the impatient gardener, that’s a dealbreaker.

## FAQ

**Q: How often do I need to reapply?**
A: Treat each urine spot once, then wait 10–14 days. If the spot doesn’t show new growth, reapply. For ongoing dog use, you’ll treat new spots as they appear — roughly every few weeks depending on your dog’s habits.

**Q: Is it safe for all grass types?**
A: It works on cool-season and warm-season grasses. The active ingredients are grass-safe when mixed correctly. If you have a brand-new sod lawn, wait until the roots establish before treating.

**Q: Can I use it on spots caused by something other than dog urine?**
A: Yes. It works on fertilizer burn and general fungal spots too, since the bio-stimulant addresses the underlying soil imbalance.
