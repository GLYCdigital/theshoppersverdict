**What You Actually Get**

The 64-ounce bottle is a heavy-duty jug — roughly the size of a half-gallon milk carton. **Real-World Performance**

Here's where Sta-Flo separates itself from the grocery store spray starches. It's a liquid concentrate, so you control the stiffness. Mix it roughly 1:1 with water for a firm hold on collars and cuffs, or go heavier — up to 2 parts starch to 1 part water — for serious structural rigidity on crafts. One buyer noted, "I use this for blocking my crochet doilies and it holds the shape perfectly, even in Florida humidity." That's the consistency I see across reviews: this stuff delivers a crispness that aerosol starches simply can't touch.

For quilting, the payoff is even bigger. Pre-washing fabric with a Sta-Flo solution before cutting prevents the fraying and shifting that drives quilters insane. It stiffens the weave just enough to make rotary cutting precise. Multiple reviewers specifically called out this use case, with one saying, "My patchwork pieces stay put now. No more wrestling with slippery fabric."

**Where It Falls Short**

Let's be honest about the downsides. The scent is the most common complaint — it's a distinct, chemical-adjacent smell that lingers after ironing. It fades, but not immediately. If you're sensitive to fragrances, plan to air items out overnight.

The second issue is user error, but it's worth flagging: if you don't dilute properly or you apply it too thick, you'll get white residue and flaking on dark fabrics. The fix is simple — always mix thoroughly and don't oversaturate — but the product doesn't include clear dilution instructions on the front label, which trips up first-time buyers.

**FAQ**

**Q: Can I use this in a steam iron?**  
A: Yes, but dilute it first (at least 1:1 with water) and use a clean spray bottle. Undiluted starch will clog the iron's steam vents and leave buildup.

**Q: Does it work on dark fabrics without leaving marks?**  
A: It can, but only if you dilute properly and iron while the fabric is damp but not dripping. Test on a hidden seam first — dark colors show residue more easily.

**Q: How long does one bottle last?**  
A: For average household use — a few shirts and craft projects weekly — expect 4-6 months. Heavy crafters using it for quilt basting might go through it in 2-3 months.
