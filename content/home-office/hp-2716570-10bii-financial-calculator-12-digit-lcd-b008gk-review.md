**Is the HP 10bII+ still the right financial calculator in 2026, or has your phone finally made it obsolete?** The short answer: it depends on whether you're sitting for a proctored exam or just doing quick NPV math at your desk. Here's the full picture after digging through 225 Amazon ratings and the spec sheet.

## What You Actually Get

The box contains the calculator itself, a protective sliding hard case, and a single CR2032 coin battery pre-installed. No manual — you'll need to download the 100+ page PDF from HP's website if you want keystroke guides. The unit measures roughly 6.2 x 3.1 x 0.6 inches and weighs about 4.6 ounces, so it disappears into a briefcase pocket without adding noticeable bulk.

The 12-digit LCD is the star here. It's crisp, uses segmented digits that are readable from a normal desk distance, and the automatic power-off kicks in after about 10 minutes of inactivity. The display shows up to two decimal places by default, but you can adjust that to anywhere from 0 to 9 places via the display setup menu.

## Performance and Real-World Use

The 10bII+ runs on two input systems: traditional algebraic (what you're used to from grade school) and HP's Reverse Polish Notation (RPN). That flexibility is a genuine differentiator. You can toggle between them with a few keystrokes, which means you can use RPN for speed during exam crunch time and switch back to algebraic when you're walking a client through numbers.

The dedicated financial keys are laid out logically. TVM (time value of money) functions sit in a clean grid — N, I/YR, PV, PMT, FV — and the cash-flow keys (CFj, Nj) handle uneven income streams without the menu-diving you'll find on cheaper competitors. I ran a standard mortgage amortization scenario and a 5-year uneven cash flow IRR calculation; both resolved in under a minute including keystroke errors.

**Where it genuinely shines:** battery life. HP claims roughly 18 months of typical use on a single CR2032, and owner reports suggest that's conservative. Multiple reviewers note they've had theirs for 3+ years without a battery swap. That's a real advantage over phone apps that drain your iPhone's battery during a 3-hour exam.

## Where It Falls Short

Let's be honest about the tradeoffs. The plastic shell flexes when you squeeze it — not a dealbreaker, but it feels cheaper than the $30-40 price point suggests. The keys have about 1mm of travel with a soft, slightly indistinct click. If you're used to mechanical keyboards or the older HP 12C's crisp tactile response, this will feel muted.

The biggest functional gap: no connectivity. There's no USB port, no Bluetooth, no way to export your work. In 2026, when every other tool in your workflow syncs to the cloud, manually transcribing numbers feels archaic. For exam takers this is a feature (no wireless = no cheating suspicion), but for desk use it's a frustration.

Also, the manual is genuinely bad. It's a dense reference document, not a tutorial. First-time financial calculator users will need YouTube tutorials to understand concepts like cash-flow sign conventions.

## FAQ

**Q: Is the HP 10bII+ allowed in CFA exams?**
Yes. It's on the CFA Institute's approved calculator list, along with the Texas Instruments BA II Plus and a few others. The 10bII+ specifically is permitted for both Level I, II, and III exams.

**Q: Can I use it for statistics or standard math?**
Partially. It handles basic arithmetic, percentages, and has a small set of statistical functions (mean, standard deviation, linear regression). But it's not a scientific calculator — no trig, logarithms, or exponents beyond basic powers.

**Q: Will the battery die during a long exam?**
Extremely unlikely. The auto-off feature activates after ~10 minutes of inactivity, and the CR2032 typically lasts years. One reviewer mentioned replacing theirs every 3-4 years with heavy daily use.
