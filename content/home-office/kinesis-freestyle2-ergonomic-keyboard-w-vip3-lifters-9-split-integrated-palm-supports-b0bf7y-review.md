If your wrists have been sending you increasingly urgent warning messages after eight-hour typing sessions, the KINESIS Freestyle2 with VIP3 lifters is the kind of product that makes you wonder why every keyboard isn't built this way. But at roughly $150–$180 depending on the day, it's also a specialized tool that demands you know exactly what you're buying.

**What's actually in the box**

You get the split keyboard itself — two independent halves connected by a thick, sturdy cable — plus the VIP3 lifters, which are the real differentiator here. These aren't the flimsy plastic flip-out legs you find on a $20 Dell board. They're substantial wedge-shaped risers that attach magnetically to the underside of each half, allowing you to tent the keyboard anywhere from flat to a steep 30 degrees. The 9" S model means the two halves come separated at 9 inches by default, which puts your hands at roughly shoulder width for most adults.

The keyboard runs on two AAA batteries (included) and connects via a 2.4GHz USB dongle. There's also a Bluetooth version, but this particular listing is the dongle model.

**How it actually performs**

The tenting is the headline act, and it delivers. When you angle the keyboard so your palms face inward at about 20 degrees, your forearms stop pronating — that twisting motion that compresses the median nerve and causes carpal tunnel issues. It took me about two days to adjust, and by day three I stopped thinking about it entirely, which is exactly what you want from ergonomic gear.

The keys themselves use low-force scissor switches. They're not mechanical — don't expect that satisfying thock — but they're crisp, quiet, and require noticeably less pressure than a standard membrane keyboard. If you're a heavy typist who bottoms out, you'll appreciate the reduced impact.

One thing the product images don't fully convey: the integrated palm supports are genuinely integrated. They're molded into the keyboard halves, not separate pads. That means you can't remove them or swap in a gel rest. For some users, this creates an awkward forced angle if you prefer floating your wrists. It's a design choice that works well for some and not others — read the Amazon reviews and you'll see both camps.

**What buyers consistently praise**

The recurring theme in the 210 ratings (4.2 stars) is pain relief. One verified buyer wrote: *"I was skeptical, but after three weeks my wrist pain is almost gone. The tenting takes some getting used to, but it works."* Another noted: *"The 9-inch separation is the only split keyboard that actually put my hands at shoulder width without buying extra parts."* The build quality also gets consistent props — this thing feels solid, with no flex in the chassis even when you're typing aggressively.

**Where it falls short**

Let's be honest about the tradeoffs. There's no backlighting, which is annoying if you work in dim environments. There are zero programmable keys — you can't remap anything without third-party software that doesn't officially exist for this board. And the fixed palm supports are a genuine gamble. If your hands are on the smaller side, the supports might push your wrists into an uncomfortable extension.

Also worth noting: the 9" separation is a fixed distance. You can't adjust it wider or narrower — the cable between the halves is a set length. The VIP3 lifters give you tenting, not separation flexibility. If you want adjustable separation, you'd need to look at the Freestyle Pro or a different brand entirely.

**Who should buy this**

This is for desk workers with early-stage wrist discomfort who want a proven, research-backed ergonomic solution without the $400 price tag of a fully split programmable board. It's also great for anyone who's tried a one-piece "ergonomic" keyboard with a fixed split and found it didn't go far enough. If you're a gamer or someone who needs macro keys, backlighting, or mechanical switches, this isn't the board for you.

**FAQ**

**Q: Is the VIP3 lifter worth it over the standard Freestyle2?**
A: Yes, if you have any wrist discomfort. The lifters are what actually reduce pronation. Without them, the Freestyle2 is just a split keyboard. With them, it becomes a therapeutic tool. The price difference is roughly $30–40, and it's the most important feature this keyboard offers.

**Q: Can I use this with a Mac?**
A: The keyboard works with Macs, but there's no dedicated Mac modifier key — you'll use the Windows key as Command. It's functional but not elegant. If you're a Mac-only user, check the Bluetooth version which has better Mac compatibility.

**Q: How loud is it in an open office?**
A: Very quiet. The scissor switches produce a soft thock, not a click. You'll hear more noise from your coworkers' mechanical keyboards than from this. It's a good choice for shared workspaces.

**The Verdict**

**Buy it if:** You're experiencing wrist pain, you want adjustable tenting without buying a $400 split keyboard, and you don't care about backlighting or programmability. The VIP3 lifters genuinely work, the build quality is excellent, and it's proven to help with discomfort.

**Skip it if:** You need a backlit keyboard, you want mechanical switches, you have smaller hands, or you're looking for a budget option. This is a specialized ergonomic tool, not a general-purpose keyboard.

**Rating: 4.2/5** — It does exactly what it's designed to do, and it does it well. The compromises are real, but for its core purpose — reducing wrist strain — it's hard to beat at this price point.
