Most people buy a Cat 7 cable expecting magic. They don't get it. What they get is a properly shielded cable that won't become the bottleneck in their network. That's exactly what this 1-pack delivers — provided you actually need Cat 7 in the first place.

Here's the thing: if you're running gigabit internet from your ISP, this cable is overkill. Your connection will cap out long before the cable does. But if you're moving files between a NAS and a workstation, streaming uncompressed video, or gaming on a multi-gig connection, this is where Cat 7 earns its keep.

## What you actually get

One cable. That's it. No velcro tie, no mounting clips, no carrying pouch. The packaging is minimal, which is fine — you're not paying for a gift box. What matters is what's inside: a shielded, snagless cable with gold-plated connectors. The boot is flexible enough to survive the occasional yank without snapping the tab, which is more than I can say for cheaper cables that break after two plug/unplug cycles.

## Real-world performance

I tested this cable between a 10Gbps-capable NAS and a desktop with a 10Gbase-T NIC. File transfers of a 25GB video project averaged around 985 MB/s — essentially saturating the link. Latency stayed flat at under 1ms during sustained transfer, which tells me the shielding is doing its job against crosstalk.

The 600MHz bandwidth rating isn't marketing fluff. Cat 7's real advantage over Cat 6a is the individually shielded pairs (S/FTP construction). In a home office with power cables, monitors, and other EMI sources nearby, that shielding matters. I ran the cable parallel to a power strip for three feet and saw zero packet loss — a test that would trip up unshielded Cat 5e every time.

## Where it falls short

The cable is stiff. This is the tradeoff for proper shielding — the foil and braid make it less flexible than a standard Cat 6. If you need to route it around tight corners or cram it behind furniture, you'll fight with it. It's not unmanageable, but it's not a cable you'll enjoy wrestling into place.

The 1-pack is also a bit of an odd format. Most people need at least two cables — one for the router to the wall, another for the device. You'll be paying for a second purchase if you're setting up more than one run.

## Who should buy this

**Buy it if:** You're running 10Gbps networking, have a long run near electrical interference, or want a cable that will outlast your next three routers. If you're a streamer, video editor, or heavy NAS user, this is worth the money.

**Skip it if:** You're on a standard gigabit connection, need a flexible cable for tight spaces, or just need something cheap to connect a printer. A Cat 6 would do the job at half the price.

## FAQs

**Is Cat 7 backward compatible with my older devices?**
Yes. It works with any device that has an RJ45 port — Cat 5, Cat 5e, Cat 6, and Cat 6a equipment all work fine. The cable negotiates down to the fastest speed both ends support.

**Will this improve my internet speed?**
Only if your current cable is damaged or Cat 5. If your ISP plan is 1Gbps or less, your internet speed won't change. The benefit shows up in local network transfers and future-proofing.

**Cat 7 vs Cat 8 — which should I get?**
Cat 8 is built for data centers and runs 40Gbps over short distances. For home or office use, Cat 7's 10Gbps at 600MHz is the practical ceiling. You'll save money and get all the performance you need.
