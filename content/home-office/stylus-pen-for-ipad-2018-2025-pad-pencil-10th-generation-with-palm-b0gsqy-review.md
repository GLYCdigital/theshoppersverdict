If you've priced an Apple Pencil lately, you know the sting. The 2nd gen runs $129; the USB-C version is $79. This stylus — hovering around a fraction of that cost — promises 90% of the experience for people who primarily write, annotate, and sketch. After spending a week with it across an iPad 10th gen and an iPad Pro, here's the honest breakdown.

**What you actually get**

The box contains the pen itself, a USB-C charging cable, and two spare tips. The pen is 166mm long and weighs about 13.5 grams — noticeably lighter than the Apple Pencil 2, which some will prefer for long writing sessions. The matte aluminum body has a flat edge that prevents rolling, a small touch-capacitive button near the tip, and an LED indicator that shows green above 30% battery and red below.

**Performance and real-world use**

The headline feature is palm rejection, and it works. You rest your hand on the screen, write naturally, and the iPad doesn't register phantom touches. It pairs automatically via Bluetooth when you tap the top — no settings digging, no app installation. Latency is the real surprise here: it's close enough to the Apple Pencil that you won't notice the difference unless you're doing rapid, precise strokes.

Battery life is genuinely impressive. I used it for roughly four hours of mixed note-taking and annotation daily, and it lasted five days before the LED dropped to red. A 30-minute USB-C charge brought it back to full. That's better real-world endurance than the Apple Pencil 2, which I've found needs topping up every couple of days with similar use.

**What buyers consistently praise**

The 4.6-star rating across 229 reviews reflects solid consensus. One verified buyer noted: "I was skeptical about a non-Apple stylus, but the palm rejection is flawless on my daughter's iPad 10th gen. She uses it for school all day and charges it once a week." Another mentioned: "The weight feels better than the real Pencil — less fatiguing during long study sessions." The tilt function also gets frequent mention — you can shade and angle strokes naturally in apps like Procreate and Notability, which matters more for sketchers than most budget pens deliver.

**Where it falls short**

The big omission is pressure sensitivity. If you're a digital artist who relies on varying line weight by pressing harder, this pen won't do it. Period. It's a binary input — the tip registers contact, not force. Apps can simulate brush dynamics via tilt, but it's not the same.

Also, the magnetic attachment only works with iPad Pro and Air models. On the 10th gen (which is likely your target if you're reading this), there's no magnetic strip to hold it, so you'll be fishing it out of your bag. It's a minor inconvenience, but worth knowing.

**Who should buy this**

- **Students and note-takers** who use an iPad for class, meetings, or journaling — this is the sweet spot. Full palm rejection plus long battery at this price is unbeatable.
- **Casual sketchers** who want tilt support and precision without spending Apple Pencil money.
- **Parents** buying a stylus for a kid's iPad — the replaceable tips and robust build survive drops better than Apple's fragile design.

**Skip it if** you're a professional illustrator who needs pressure sensitivity, or if you specifically want the magnetic snap-and-charge convenience of the Apple Pencil 2 on a Pro model.

**FAQ**

**Q: Does it work with the iPad 10th generation specifically?**
A: Yes. It's compatible with all iPads from 2018 onward, including the 10th gen (USB-C models). The pen connects via Bluetooth and works with the standard Apple Pencil API, so any app that supports Apple Pencil will recognize it.

**Q: How do I check the battery level?**
A: The LED near the top shows green (above 30%) or red (below 30%). For a precise percentage, open the Bluetooth settings on your iPad — the pen appears as a connected device with a battery indicator there.

**Q: Will I need to replace the tip eventually?**
A: Yes, but that's expected with any stylus. The included spare tip is a nice touch. Replacement tips for this pen run about $10 for a two-pack, and they're easy to swap by twisting the old one off.
