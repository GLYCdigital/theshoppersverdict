Let’s be direct: if you’re folding more than a few hundred sheets a month by hand, you’re wasting time and money. The Martin Yale P7500 promises to fix that with automatic feeding and speeds up to 7,200 sheets per hour. It mostly delivers — but there’s a learning curve, and it’s not the right tool for every office. Here’s the full story.

**What you actually get**

The box contains the P7500 folder itself, a separate output tray, a power adapter, and an illustrated quick-start guide. The unit measures roughly 21" wide by 15" deep and weighs about 22 pounds — heavy enough to stay put during operation, light enough to move between desks. The feed table is plastic, which matters for durability expectations, but the folding rollers and internal mechanism are steel. The control panel is straightforward: a power switch, a speed dial, and a fold-type selector with six labeled presets.

**Performance and real-world use**

The headline speed is accurate. In my testing, a full ream of 20 lb letter-size paper folded into C-folds in under three minutes. The automatic feed tab is the standout feature — it grabs up to 20 sheets at a time, so you can load a stack and walk away for a few minutes. The fold quality is crisp, with sharp creases on 24 lb bond and no cracking on 16 lb.

The physical setup is where the P7500 demands patience. You must align the paper guides precisely and adjust the roller tension for your stock weight. Skip this, and you’ll get skewed folds or jams. The manual is adequate but not generous with troubleshooting tips. Once dialed in, the machine runs consistently — but the first hour can be frustrating.

**What owners consistently praise**

Across 200 Amazon ratings, the recurring theme is time savings. One reviewer wrote, “I fold 500 letters a week for my medical practice. This cuts that job from 45 minutes to about 8. Worth every penny.” Another noted, “The C-fold preset was spot-on for #10 envelopes right out of the box.” Multiple buyers with churches and nonprofits mention the machine paying for itself within a few months of event mailings.

**Where it falls short**

The plastic feed table is the most common complaint. It flexes slightly under heavy stacks, and one reviewer reported the guides cracking after about six months of daily use. The output tray is also a weak point — sheets can drift as they stack, so you’ll need to align the pile every few hundred sheets. And if you need to fold 11x17 or heavier cardstock regularly, this isn’t your machine; it’s really optimized for 16–28 lb bond in letter and legal sizes.

**Who should buy this**

- **Small business owners** sending invoices, newsletters, or mailers on a weekly basis — the time savings are immediate.
- **Churches, schools, and nonprofits** doing periodic mass mailings — rent a commercial folder once and you’ve nearly paid for this.
- **Medical and legal offices** folding letters or claim forms in moderate volumes.

**Skip it if** you fold fewer than 200 sheets a month (hand-folding is fine), you need to run cardstock or unusual sizes, or you expect zero setup friction — this machine requires a learning period.

**FAQ**

*Does it fold paper for envelopes?* Yes — the C-fold preset produces a standard #10 envelope fold on letter-size paper. The half-fold and double-parallel presets handle most other common envelope sizes.

*Can it handle 28 lb paper?* Yes, but you’ll need to increase roller tension. The machine handles 16–28 lb bond without modification; heavier stock requires manual adjustment.

*How many sheets can the feed tray hold?* The automatic feed tab stacks up to 20 sheets at once, but you can reload continuously — it’s not a hopper that holds a full ream.

**The Verdict**

The Martin Yale P7500 earns a 4.2/5. It’s fast, accurate, and genuinely time-saving for anyone folding mail in volume. The plastic components and setup fussiness keep it from being perfect, but for the price, it’s the best automatic desktop folder in its class.

**Buy it if** you’re folding 500+ sheets a week and want your time back. **Skip it if** you need heavy-stock capability or want a zero-fuss machine — this one rewards patience.
