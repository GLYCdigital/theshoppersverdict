Here's the thing about metallic markers: most of them are glittery garbage that smears the moment you look at them. SHARPIE Metallics are not that. With a 4.4-star average across 220 ratings, these are the rare permanent markers that actually deliver on the "metallic" promise—but they're not without quirks you need to know before buying.

## What You Actually Get

The set includes the standard SHARPIE Metallics lineup—gold, silver, and bronze (though some configurations include copper and a metallic blue). Each marker uses a classic bullet tip, which is a deliberate choice: fine enough for lettering on envelopes, robust enough to color in larger areas on posters or craft projects. The ink is permanent, oil-based, and explicitly designed for non-porous surfaces.

## Real-World Performance

I tested these on black cardstock, clear glass, anodized aluminum, and a white ceramic mug. The standout performance is on dark surfaces. The metallic pigment is genuinely opaque—not that translucent shimmer you get from cheaper brands. On black paper, the gold reads as actual gold leaf, not yellow-gray disappointment. One Amazon reviewer put it plainly: *"I've bought every metallic marker out there, and these are the first ones that don't look like silver crayon on dark paper."*

The quick-dry claim holds up. On glass and metal, the ink sets in roughly 20-30 seconds. You can handle the surface without transferring pigment. That alone puts these ahead of craft-store alternatives that stay tacky for minutes.

The bullet tip is a workhorse. After heavy use on rough surfaces like untreated wood and cardboard, the tip held its shape—no fraying, no flattening. SHARPIE's nib quality is consistent with their standard permanent markers, which is exactly what you want.

## Where It Falls Short

Honesty time. On white or light-colored paper, the metallic effect nearly disappears. Gold and bronze look almost identical until the ink fully dries—and even then, the distinction is subtle. If your primary use is writing on white copy paper, these will disappoint. They're surface-specific tools.

The pigment separation is the bigger issue. The metallic particles settle in the barrel quickly. If you don't shake the marker vigorously before each use—and I mean *vigorously*—you'll get watery, translucent streaks that look nothing like the swatch on the packaging. This is common with metallic markers, but it's still a friction point for everyday use.

## Who Should Buy These

- **Crafters and DIYers** working with dark paper, glassware, or painted surfaces—these are your best friend for personalized gifts and signage
- **Office workers** labeling dark file folders, binders, or equipment—the permanence on plastic and metal is genuinely reliable
- **Artists** who need opaque metallic accents on mixed-media pieces

**Skip these if** you're a teacher or student writing on standard whiteboards or copy paper. The metallic effect is wasted there, and you'd be better served by standard SHARPIE permanent markers at a lower price point.

## FAQ

**Do these work on plastic?**
Yes, but test first. Hard plastics (polypropylene, ABS) hold the ink well. Soft, flexible plastics like Ziploc bags will cause the ink to crack and peel once bent.

**How long do they last before drying out?**
With the caps replaced properly and regular shaking, expect 3-4 months of weekly use. The oil-based formula is slower to dry out than water-based metallic markers.

**Are they waterproof?**
Once fully cured (24 hours), the ink is water-resistant. It won't run under light moisture, but prolonged soaking will eventually degrade the metallic layer.
