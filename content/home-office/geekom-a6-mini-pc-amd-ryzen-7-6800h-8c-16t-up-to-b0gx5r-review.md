Open the box and the first thing you notice is the weight. The GEEKOM A6 isn't featherlight plastic like budget mini PCs — it's a solid aluminum brick that feels like it costs more than it does. That's a good sign, because what's inside matters more.

This isn't a basic office PC. The Ryzen 7 6800H packs 8 cores and 16 threads boosting to 4.7GHz, and the Radeon 680M integrated graphics punch well above their weight class. For a workspace machine, that's overkill in the best way.

**What you actually get**

The box includes the A6 unit itself (measuring roughly 4.5 x 4.5 x 1.5 inches), a 120W power adapter, an HDMI cable, a VESA mount bracket with screws, and a quick-start guide. No keyboard or mouse — this is a barebones system, so budget for those separately. There's also no Windows license included, which surprises some buyers. You're getting the hardware; you supply the OS. That's worth knowing before checkout.

**Real-world performance**

I ran this as a daily driver for a week — 30+ browser tabs, Slack, video calls, and occasional light photo editing in GIMP. It never broke a sweat. The 8-core chip handles productivity workloads with genuine headroom. Where it gets interesting is the graphics. The Radeon 680M can play esports titles like Rocket League at 1080p medium settings around 60fps, and it handles 4K video playback without stutter.

One buyer noted: *"I was skeptical about integrated graphics, but this thing runs my photo editing workflow faster than my old desktop with a dedicated GPU."* That tracks with what I saw.

**Build quality and thermals**

The aluminum chassis doubles as a heatsink, and it's effective — idle temps sit around 45°C. Under sustained load, expect fan noise. It's not jet-engine loud, but in a silent office you'll hear it spin up during long renders or gaming sessions. The fan curve could be more aggressive about cooling versus noise; it prioritizes temperature, which is fine for longevity but noticeable.

**Where it falls short**

The port layout is my biggest gripe. The front panel has two USB-A ports and a USB4 Type-C, but no SD card slot and no headphone jack. Photographers and video editors will need adapters. The rear hosts dual HDMI 2.0, two more USB-A, 2.5G Ethernet, and the USB4 port — but the Wi-Fi antenna connectors sit awkwardly close to the HDMI ports, making cable management fiddly.

Also, 16GB of RAM is soldered. If you buy the base model, you're locked in. The 32GB version costs more but future-proofs better. Given the soldered memory, I'd recommend the higher-RAM config if your budget allows.

**Who should buy this**

- **Remote workers and students** who want a compact, quiet machine that handles video calls and document work without lag
- **Light gamers** who stick to esports titles or older AAA games at 1080p — the 680M genuinely holds up
- **Multi-monitor office users** who need triple 4K output from a tiny footprint

**Skip it if** you need heavy 4K video editing with effects, want a silent fan curve under load, or prefer upgradable RAM.

**FAQ**

**Q: Does it come with Windows pre-installed?**
A: No. This is a barebones unit. You'll need to install Windows 11 or Linux yourself. The hardware supports both well.

**Q: Can it handle 4K video editing?**
A: Light editing in DaVinci Resolve or Premiere basics — yes. Heavy effects, color grading, or long timelines — no. This is a productivity machine with gaming potential, not a workstation.

**Q: Is the fan always loud?**
A: No. It's silent at idle and quiet during typical office work. Only under sustained CPU/GPU load (gaming, rendering) does it become audible.

**The Verdict**

**Buy it if** you want desktop-class performance in a footprint that disappears on a desk, and you don't mind supplying your own OS. The soldered RAM is the main compromise, but for most office workloads, 16GB is plenty for years.

**Skip it if** you need silent operation under load, want expandable memory, or expect a full desktop experience with optical drive and card reader.

**Rating: 4.2/5** — Excellent value for the performance, held back by port layout quirks and soldered RAM.
