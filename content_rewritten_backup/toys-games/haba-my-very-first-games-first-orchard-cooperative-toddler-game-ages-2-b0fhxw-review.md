**The Short Version**

Most toddler games are garbage. They're plastic, break within a week, or require a PhD in patience to set up. HABA's First Orchard is none of those things. It's the rare toddler game that actually respects its audience — and their parents' floors.

This is the classic "Orchard" game scaled down for little hands, and it's been a nursery staple in Germany for decades. The 4.8-star average across 240 ratings isn't hype. It's parents who've played this 400 times and still haven't thrown it out the window.

---

**What's Actually in the Box**

You get four wooden fruits (red apple, green apple, yellow pear, blue plum), each with a little wooden "stem" that fits into the fruit base. There's a wooden raven, a wooden die with colored symbols, a cloth-lined wooden basket, and a game board that folds into three sections. Everything fits back into a sturdy cardboard box roughly the size of a hardcover book.

The fruit pieces are about 2 inches tall — chunky enough for a 2-year-old to grasp but not so big they're unwieldy. The game board shows an orchard with four trees and a path leading to the raven.

---

**How It Plays**

Each player takes a turn rolling the die. Roll a fruit color, pick that fruit from the tree and put it in the basket. Roll the basket symbol, pick any fruit. Roll the raven, and the raven hops one step closer to the orchard.

Here's the twist: everyone wins together if you collect all the fruit before the raven reaches the orchard. Everyone loses together if the raven gets there first.

That cooperative mechanic is the genius of this game. There's no "winner" and "loser" — which means no toddler meltdowns when someone doesn't win. The emotional stakes are shared. When the raven wins, kids learn to handle disappointment as a team. When they win, they learn to celebrate together.

The difficulty is adjustable. Start with just the fruit and die for the youngest players. Add the raven when they're ready. Older kids can play the "advanced" variant with an extra rule that adds a bit more strategy.

---

**Build Quality and Real-World Use**

This is where HABA earns its reputation. The wood is solid beech — not the cheap pine you find in most wooden toys. The paint is durable and doesn't chip or peel, even after being dropped, thrown, and gnawed on. The cloth basket is stitched well and holds up to constant use.

Parents in the Amazon reviews consistently mention the durability. One reviewer said their "2-year-old threw the fruit pieces across the room repeatedly" and they "still look brand new." Another noted they've had the game for "two kids and three years" with no wear.

The raven piece is the one design quibble. It's a single carved piece of wood, about 1.5 inches tall, with painted eyes. It's charming, but it's small enough that you'll want to keep it away from younger siblings who still put things in their mouths.

---

**Where It Falls Short**

The gameplay is simple. Very simple. For a 2-year-old, that's perfect. For a 4-year-old who's played it a dozen times, it can become repetitive. The "advanced" variant helps, but it's not going to hold a 5-year-old's attention.

The die is wooden with painted symbols — not printed stickers, which is good — but the paint can wear slightly over years of use. Not a dealbreaker, but worth noting.

The price point is higher than comparable plastic games. You're paying for the wood quality and the HABA name. If you're on a tight budget, there are cheaper options, but they won't last as long or feel as premium.

**FAQ**

**Q: Is this safe for a 2-year-old?**
A: The fruit pieces and basket are safe for 2+. The raven and die are small enough to be a choking hazard for children under 3, so supervise play or remove those pieces for the youngest players.

**Q: How long does a game take?**
A: About 5-10 minutes per round. Short enough to hold a toddler's attention, long enough to feel like a real game.

**Q: Can this be played with one child?**
A: Yes. The cooperative format works solo — the child plays "against" the raven. It's actually a great way to teach emotional regulation since the child experiences both winning and losing alone.
