## The 48-Blade Difference You Can Actually Feel

Most "tenderizers" are glorified hammers. They flatten your steak into a sad, uneven pancake and call it a day. The Jaccard takes a fundamentally different approach — instead of smashing muscle fibers, it **severs them** with 48 thin, surgical-grade stainless steel blades arranged in a grid. You press down, pull up, and repeat. That's it.

I've used this on flank steak, chicken breasts, pork shoulder, and even a stubborn round roast that usually chews like a tire. The difference isn't subtle. Those 48 blades create micro-channels that break down collagen without destroying the meat's structure. You get a tender bite **and** a proper steak shape.

## What You Actually Get

The package includes the tenderizer unit itself — handle, blade head, and a protective storage case. No marinating container, no recipe cards, no filler. The handle is a solid, ergonomic piece of ABS plastic with a rubberized grip that stays put even when your hands are slick with meat juice. The blade assembly pops off with a simple button release, which is crucial because you *will* need to clean it thoroughly.

As shown in the product image, the 48 blades are arranged in a tight 6×8 grid. Each blade is roughly 1.5 inches long — enough to penetrate thick cuts without punching all the way through a standard 1-inch steak.

## Real-World Performance

Here's where the Jaccard earns its keep. On a 1.5-inch flank steak, **three passes** (one per side, plus one extra on the thickest section) cut cooking time by about 30% and transformed a notoriously tough cut into something you could slice with a butter knife. The blades glide through raw meat with minimal resistance — you don't need to lean your body weight into it.

Cleanup is the tradeoff. Those 48 blades are fantastic at tenderizing and equally fantastic at trapping meat fibers between them. The blade assembly is dishwasher-safe, but I've found hand-washing with a stiff brush under hot water gets the crevices cleaner. It adds maybe two minutes to cleanup. Worth it.

## Where It Falls Short

The blades are the weak point. They're sharp and effective, but they're also **thin and unforgiving**. Hit a bone or a cutting board with too much force and you'll bend a blade — which ruins the entire head, not just one needle. Replacement heads aren't sold separately on this listing, so a mistake means buying a whole new unit. Be disciplined: never use this on bone-in meat, and always tenderize on a plastic or wood board, never directly on a hard surface.

Also, this tenderizes *texture* only. It does nothing for flavor. If you're working with a bland cut, you'll still need a marinade or a dry rub to make it taste like anything. The micro-channels do help marinades penetrate faster, but they don't replace them.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I use this on chicken with bones?**
A: No. Keep it to boneless cuts. Hitting a bone will bend the blades and ruin the unit. Debone first.

**Q: Does it actually make meat more tender, or is it a gimmick?**
A: It's legit. The blades physically sever connective tissue. You'll notice the difference on the first use, especially with flank steak, round steak, or pork shoulder.

**Q: How many times should I press?**
A: Start with 2–3 passes per side for a 1-inch cut. More passes = more tender, but you'll lose structural integrity past 5–6 passes. Over-tenderized meat turns mushy.
