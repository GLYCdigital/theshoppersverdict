If you've priced out a commercial juice extractor lately, you already know they run $200-$500. The VIVOHOME Stainless Steel Juice Steamer takes a different approach: it's a stovetop steamer that uses heat, not centrifugal force, to pull juice out of fruit. At roughly half the price of a countertop extractor, it's worth a hard look if you're batch-processing grapes, apples, or tomatoes.

## What You Actually Get

The box contains four stainless steel pieces: a large 11.8-quart water pot, a juice collector pan with a spout, a perforated steamer basket, and a domed lid. As shown in the product image, it stacks into a three-tier column — water boils at the bottom, steam rises through the fruit in the middle, and juice drips down into the collector pan. The whole assembly measures about 16 inches tall when stacked, so make sure your stove has clearance above it.

## How It Performs in Real Use

The core advantage here is simplicity. You load the basket with prepared fruit — washed, stemmed, and chopped into chunks — fill the bottom pot with water, and let it steam for 45-90 minutes depending on what you're juicing. The steam gently breaks down cell walls, and gravity does the rest. There's no pulp in your juice, no motor to clean, and no heat-generating friction that can cook the juice's flavor.

The 18/8 stainless steel is thick enough to distribute heat evenly across the bottom pot, which matters when you're running a boil for over an hour. The generously sized juice pan holds about 8 quarts of liquid, and the spout pours cleanly into mason jars without dribbling down the side. One buyer noted, "I made 6 quarts of grape juice from 12 pounds of grapes in one afternoon — no straining, no mess."

The steamer basket is deep and wide, holding roughly 10-12 pounds of fruit per batch. That's a meaningful advantage over electric juicers, which typically require you to feed fruit through one piece at a time.

## What Buyers Consistently Praise

Across the 235 ratings averaging 4.7 stars, a few themes repeat. First: durability. Multiple reviewers mention using it for years without any warping or rust, even with frequent canning-season use. Second: the clean juice yield. Because steam extraction doesn't crush seeds or skins, the resulting juice is clearer and less bitter than what you get from a masticating juicer. Third: ease of cleanup. There's no pulp basket to scrub and no filter screen to unclog — just rinse the tiers with warm soapy water.

## Where It Falls Short

The perforated basket has small holes that work great for firm fruit but clog with soft berries or overripe peaches. One reviewer warned, "Strawberries turned to mush and blocked the holes — I had to push the puree through with a spoon." You can mitigate this by lining the basket with cheesecloth, but that adds a step and a consumable.

There's also no temperature gauge, which is a real limitation for canning. Safe water-bath canning requires juice at 212°F, and steam juicers can't always tell you where you are temperature-wise. You'll want a separate candy thermometer clipped to the pot.

Finally, the 11.8-quart capacity means you'll need a large stovetop burner — it overhangs smaller electric coils, which slows the boil significantly.

## Who Should Buy This

**Buy it if:** You're a home canner who processes seasonal fruit in 10+ pound batches, you want juice with no added water or sugar, or you're tired of cleaning pulp out of a centrifugal juicer. It's also ideal for making grape juice, apple juice, and tomato puree for sauces.

**Skip it if:** You want a glass of fresh orange juice every morning (this takes 45+ minutes per batch), you're short on cabinet space, or you need precise temperature readings for pressure canning.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I use this on an induction cooktop?**
A: Yes — the stainless steel is magnetic, so it works on induction, gas, and electric coils. Just ensure the pot fully covers the element for even heating.

**Q: How much juice does it actually produce?**
A: Expect roughly 60-70% juice yield by weight. Ten pounds of grapes gives about 6-7 quarts of juice. Yield varies with fruit water content — watermelons and citrus do better, apples and pears slightly less.

**Q: Does the juice need to be boiled after steaming?**
A: The steam process heats juice to around 180-200°F, which kills most pathogens. For shelf-stable canning, you still need to process filled jars in a water bath at 212°F for the recommended time per your recipe.
