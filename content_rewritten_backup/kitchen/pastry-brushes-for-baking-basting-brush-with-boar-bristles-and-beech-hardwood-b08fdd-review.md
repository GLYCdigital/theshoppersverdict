If you've been baking with a silicone pastry brush, you already know the frustration: it smears egg wash instead of painting it, and half the butter stays stuck between those rubbery fingers. This boar-bristle brush from the "Pastry Brushes for Baking" line takes a traditional approach that solves those problems — but not without a few quirks of its own.

## What You Actually Get

This is a classic wooden-handled brush measuring about 8 inches total. The head is packed with natural boar bristles set into a beech hardwood handle. There's a small hanging hole at the end of the handle for hook storage. No bells, no whistles — just a straight-up functional tool that looks like something your grandmother would have owned.

## Real-World Performance

The difference between this and silicone becomes obvious the moment you dip it into an egg wash. The bristles wick up liquid rather than repelling it. One dip coats a full sheet of croissants — I counted three dips for a full batch of 12. With silicone, I was dipping every two pastries and still getting patchy coverage.

The bristles are dense but flexible. They glide over delicate puff pastry without tearing, and they get into the crevices of braided breads that silicone simply can't reach. Butter distribution on pie crust edges is noticeably more even — no more puddles pooling in the crimped sections.

As shown in the product image, the boar bristles are a natural tan color and vary slightly in texture. That variation actually helps with pickup and distribution. The brush holds its shape well during use and doesn't splay out like cheaper versions.

## Where It Falls Short

Let's be honest about the downsides. Natural bristle brushes shed. Several reviewers reported losing a few hairs during the first week of use. Pre-washing in warm soapy water before first use helps, but you'll still find the occasional bristle on your pastry. Check your bakes before serving.

Cleanup requires more effort than silicone. You need to hand wash with mild soap, rinse thoroughly, and dry hanging bristle-side down. Skip proper drying and you risk mildew — a dealbreaker for some. Also, this brush is not meant for high-heat cooking. It's for basting and glazing before baking or grilling, not for brushing oil onto a screaming-hot grill grate.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I clean this brush without ruining it?**
Wash with warm water and mild dish soap immediately after use. Rinse until water runs clear, shake off excess, then hang bristle-side down to dry. Never soak it, and never put it in the dishwasher.

**Q: Will the boar bristles fall out?**
Some initial shedding is normal — wash before first use to remove loose hairs. With proper care, the bristles stay put after the first few uses. If you see excessive shedding, that's a defect.

**Q: Can I use this with hot oil or on a hot grill?**
No. This is for room-temperature or warm liquids applied before cooking. Natural bristles will scorch and melt at high temperatures. Use a metal or silicone brush for hot grill work.
