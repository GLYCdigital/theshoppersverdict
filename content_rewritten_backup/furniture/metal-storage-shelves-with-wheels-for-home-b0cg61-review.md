## What You Actually Get

The box contains four steel wire shelves, four upright posts, eight crossbars, and four locking caster wheels. Each shelf measures roughly 36 inches wide by 18 inches deep—enough for standard storage bins, small appliances, or stacked canned goods. The height adjusts in 1.5-inch increments along the posts, so you can reconfigure clearance for a stand mixer or a stack of paint cans.

The finish is epoxy-coated matte black, which resists fingerprints better than gloss options. The wire grid spacing is tight enough (about 1 inch) that you won’t lose small items like spice jars or drill bits through the gaps. Each shelf carries a rated 220-pound capacity when weight is distributed evenly—that’s not marketing fluff; multiple buyers confirm loading it with heavy bins without sagging.

## Build Quality and Real-World Rolling

The steel gauge feels substantial—not the flimsy .5mm stuff you find on $30 racks. The posts are 1-inch square tubing with a powder coat that survives scrapes from sliding boxes in and out. The locking casters are the standout feature: all four lock independently, so you can pin the rack in place on uneven garage floors. One reviewer noted, “I loaded it with three 40-pound bags of dog food and rolled it across the basement without a wobble.”

The wheels are 2-inch plastic-core casters. They glide smoothly on concrete, hardwood, and laminate. On ceramic tile with wide grout lines, you’ll hear a slight chatter—not a dealbreaker, but worth knowing if your kitchen has textured tile. The locks engage firmly, though they require a firm press with your toe; wearing sandals, that can be mildly annoying.

The recurring theme in reviews is stability. ## Where It Falls Short

Assembly is the biggest friction point. The crossbars attach with bolts that require a Phillips screwdriver—there are no pre-tapped holes, so you’ll hand-thread each bolt. It’s not hard, but it’s tedious, especially if you’re assembling two units. One reviewer warned: “Give yourself an hour, not the 20 minutes the box claims.”

The plastic-core wheels are the second compromise. They’re fine for occasional rolling, but if you plan to move this daily (like in a workshop or craft room), the plastic can develop flat spots over time. Consider upgrading to rubber casters if you’re a frequent mover. Also, the maximum height (about 72 inches) means the top shelf is unreachable for most people under 5’8” without a step stool—plan your layout accordingly.
