You’re looking at a padlock. Most people grab the cheapest laminated steel option at the hardware store and call it done. The Puroma 2.6-inch solid brass padlock costs a bit more, but it solves the two biggest complaints with budget locks: rust and jammed mechanisms. Here’s the honest breakdown after digging through 229 ratings and the spec sheet.

**What you actually get**  
The box includes one padlock and two brass keys. The lock body measures 2.6 inches across — that’s a mid-size lock, not a tiny luggage lock. The shackle is 3/8-inch thick with a 1-1/8-inch clearance, which fits standard gate hasps, shed doors, tool chests, and most locker latches. The weight feels substantial in hand; this isn’t a hollow shell.

**Build quality and real-world performance**  
The headline feature is the solid brass construction. Unlike laminated steel locks that corrode at the first sign of rain, brass doesn’t rust. That’s the core reason this lock holds up outdoors. The internal mechanism is a 5-pin cylinder — not the cheap 4-pin tumblers you find on dollar-store locks. The key turns smoothly, and the shackle snaps shut with a positive click.

Buyers consistently mention the weather resistance. One reviewer wrote, *“Left it on my shed gate all winter through snow and rain — still opens like day one.”* Another noted, *“I’ve had cheap locks seize up after one season. This one hasn’t missed a beat.”* That tracks with the brass body and the plated shackle, which resists surface rust better than raw steel.

**Where it falls short**  
Be realistic about what this lock is. The 3/8-inch shackle is *not* security-grade. A pair of 24-inch bolt cutters will snap it. If you need to secure a storage unit or a high-value trailer, spend more on a shrouded shackle lock like the Abus 83/55. This Puroma is a deterrence lock, not a fortress.

Second, the keyway is exposed. There’s no sliding dust cover, so in dusty or sandy environments, grit can work into the cylinder. Most owners didn’t report issues, but if you live in a desert or near a beach, spray the keyhole with dry lubricant every few months. Also note: the keys are brass and thin. They bend if you torque them — handle with normal care.

**Who should buy this**  
- **Homeowners with sheds, gates, or fence latches** — the weather resistance is the main draw.  
- **Boaters or RV owners** — salt air destroys steel locks; brass handles it.  
- **Anyone needing multiple locks with one key** — Puroma offers keyed-alike sets, so you can use one key for your gate, tool shed, and trailer hitch.  
- **Not for high-security needs** — if you’re locking up expensive equipment in a public area, step up to a hardened steel or shrouded shackle model.

**FAQ**  
**Q: Can I get extra keys made?**  
A: Yes. The key blank is a standard 5-pin brass blank (similar to Ilco 1058BR). Any locksmith can cut copies for a couple of dollars. Avoid the cheap online key-copying services — they often mangle brass keys.

**Q: Does the brass tarnish over time?**  
A: It will develop a patina, especially outdoors. That’s cosmetic, not structural. If you want it shiny, a quick wipe with a brass polish restores it. Many buyers actually like the aged look.

**Q: Is this lock pick-resistant?**  
A: Not particularly. The 5-pin cylinder is standard — a skilled picker can open it in under a minute. But for most residential applications, that’s irrelevant. Thieves use bolt cutters, not picks. And as noted, the shackle is the weak point anyway.

**The Verdict**  
**Buy it if** you want a dependable, rust-proof lock for outdoor gates, sheds, or any application where weather exposure kills cheap locks. The brass body and smooth 5-pin mechanism justify the price over a $5 laminated lock.

**Skip it if** you need real theft deterrence for valuables in a high-risk area. The shackle is too thin for that job.

**Rating: 4.6/5** — a genuinely solid, well-priced lock for its intended use. Just don’t oversell it as a security device.
