## What You Actually Get

The set includes five spiral extractors, sized No. 1 through No. 5. As shown in the product image, these are the classic straight-flute spiral design — not the newer left-hand drill bit style. The working range spans from tiny No. 4 screws up to 3/8-inch bolts, which covers most household and automotive jobs. Each extractor is made from heat-treated S2 steel, the same alloy used in premium screwdriver bits. The spiral flutes are sharp and deep, and the square shank at the top is designed to fit a tap handle or T-handle wrench — not a drill chuck. That's an important distinction we'll get to below.

## How It Performs in Real Use

The spiral design is the key here. As you turn the extractor counterclockwise, the spiral bites deeper into the fastener. It's a self-tightening action — the more resistance you meet, the harder it grips. This is the opposite of cheap extractors that cam out under load.

Buyers consistently report success on rusted and seized fasteners. One reviewer noted, "Pulled a broken bleeder screw out of a caliper that had been snapped off for years. Used penetrating oil, let it sit, and the No. 3 extractor grabbed on the first try." Another mentioned using the No. 4 on a stripped lug stud. The tradeoff is that the smaller sizes (No. 1 and No. 2) are thin — if you lean on them too hard on a fully seized bolt, they can snap. That's true of any extractor set, but it's worth noting before you apply maximum force.

## Where It Falls Short

The biggest complaint is the lack of a storage case. The bits come loose in a plastic bag, which is annoying when you're reaching into a drawer and grab the wrong size. The No. 1 and No. 2 bits are also easy to misplace given how small they are.

Second, the square shank design means you need a tap handle or a 5/16-inch T-handle wrench. If you don't own one, factor that into your cost — a basic tap handle runs about $10-15. You can't just chuck these into a drill, which is actually a safety feature (drill-powered extractors can snap and send metal flying), but it's a learning curve for beginners.

Third, drilling a pilot hole is still required for most extractions. The set doesn't include drill bits, so you'll need a center punch and a set of left-hand drill bits to get the best results. That's standard for this type of extractor, but it's not a one-tool solution.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I use these with a cordless drill?**
A: No. The square shank is designed for a tap handle or T-handle wrench. Using a drill risks snapping the bit and damaging the fastener further.

**Q: Do I need to drill a pilot hole first?**
A: Yes. For best results, center-punch the broken fastener, drill a pilot hole (use the extractor size chart in the listing), then insert the extractor and turn counterclockwise.

**Q: Will these work on stripped Phillips or Torx screws?**
A: Yes, as long as you can drill a small pilot hole into the screw head. For screws with the head still intact, the No. 1 or No. 2 extractor will bite into the head and back it out.
