If you've priced a dedicated sawmill lately — even a small one — you already know they start around $1,500 and climb fast. The Zozen Chainsaw Mill undercuts that by roughly 95 percent. But here's the catch: it's not a sawmill. It's a steel and aluminum frame that turns the chainsaw you already own into one. That's either a screaming deal or a recipe for disappointment, depending on what you expect.

## What's Actually in the Box

The kit ships with two 10-foot aluminum guide rails, a pair of mounting brackets that clamp onto your chainsaw's bar, and all the bolts, wing nuts, and washers needed for assembly. The brackets are machined aluminum with a steel clamping plate — not the flimsy stamped metal you see on cheaper clones. The rails measure 10 feet each, which lets you rip-cut logs up to 20 inches in diameter in a single pass. That's the headline spec, and it's accurate.

Assembly takes about 15 minutes. The wing nuts are large enough to tighten by hand, and the bracket design lets you adjust the cutting depth by sliding the whole assembly up or down the bar. You'll want a 14- to 20-inch bar on your saw; anything shorter limits your log capacity, and anything longer than 20 inches will overhang the rail brackets.

## How It Actually Performs

The key question with any chainsaw mill is whether it cuts straight. The Zozen's answer is: mostly yes, with a learning curve. The dual-bracket design holds the bar rigidly in both axes, and users consistently report clean, uniform slabs once they get the rail seated properly on the log. One reviewer put it plainly: "First cut was rough because I rushed the setup. Second log, I took my time, and the boards came out straight enough for furniture-grade work."

The cutting depth adjustability is the standout feature. You can dial in anything from a 1/2-inch slab to a 4-inch beam without tools — just loosen the wing nuts, slide, tighten. That's genuinely useful if you're alternating between firewood planks and dimensional lumber.

## Where It Falls Short

Be honest about the limitations. The zinc-plated hardware will rust if you leave the mill mounted on your saw between uses — one buyer reported surface corrosion after two weeks in a damp shed. Wipe it down or disassemble after each session.

The brackets also won't fit every chainsaw. They're designed for standard .050 and .058 gauge chains. If you're running a .063 low-profile setup — common on smaller homeowner saws — you'll need to swap chains or skip this mill. Check your bar's gauge before ordering.

And there's the unavoidable reality: this is manual milling. You're pushing a chainsaw through logs by hand. It's physically demanding work, and it's slower than a powered mill. If you're milling more than a few logs a year, the labor cost will eventually justify a real sawmill.

## Who Should Buy This

This mill is perfect for the homesteader cutting firewood who wants to turn a few choice logs into slabs for shelving, tabletops, or timber framing. It's also ideal for the budget-conscious woodworker who needs occasional dimensional lumber and doesn't want to rent a mill or pay sawyer fees. The person who should skip it? Anyone planning to mill more than 20 logs a year, or anyone expecting finished-grade lumber without additional planing and jointing.

## FAQ: Three Questions Buyers Actually Ask

**Does it work with a 16-inch bar?**
Yes, but you'll be limited to logs around 12–14 inches in diameter. The 20-inch bar lets you use the full rail capacity. If you're buying a new saw for this, go with at least an 18-inch bar.

**Can I use this with an electric chainsaw?**
Technically yes, but electric saws lack the torque for sustained rip cuts. Stick with a gas saw rated at 40cc or higher. Buyers who tried electric reported bogging down on anything harder than pine.

**Will the rails bend over time?**
The aluminum rails are surprisingly stiff, and no reviewer reported bending under normal use. But they're not indestructible — don't store them under heavy weight or drop them on concrete.
