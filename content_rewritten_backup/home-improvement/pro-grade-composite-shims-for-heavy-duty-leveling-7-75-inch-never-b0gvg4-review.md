**What you actually get**

The package contains 7.75-inch composite shims, roughly 1.5 inches longer than standard 6-inch wood shims. Each shim has a tapered design that lets you fine-tune gaps from nearly zero up to about 3/8 inch. The composite material is dense — noticeably heavier than cedar — with a slight texture on both faces. As shown in the product image, they're uniform in color and thickness, which tells you the manufacturing tolerances are tighter than what you get from a random bundle of wood shims at the hardware store.

**Performance and build quality**

The real test is how they handle load. Wood shims compress over time — that's physics. Composite shims don't. Multiple reviewers noted they've used these for leveling heavy cabinets and door frames and come back months later to find zero settling. One contractor wrote: "I used these under a 300-pound workbench and they haven't budged in six months. Wood shims would have crushed flat by now."

The 7.75-inch length is the standout feature. Extra length means more surface area contacting your workpiece, which translates to better load distribution and less chance of the shim tipping or walking out from under a heavy load. For leveling appliances, windows, or door jambs, that extra inch and three-quarters makes a real difference.

The textured surface prevents the shim from sliding on smooth concrete or tile during installation. That's a small detail, but when you're balancing a heavy cabinet with one hand and trying to seat a shim with the other, grip matters.

**What buyers consistently praise**

The dominant theme in positive reviews is durability. One buyer said: "These are the last shims I'll ever need to buy. They don't split, they don't rot, and they hold their shape under pressure." Another praised their use outdoors: "I used them to level a shed foundation and left them exposed to rain. Six months later they look brand new."

The resistance to moisture is worth emphasizing. Cedar shims absorb water, swell, and eventually rot. These composite shims are essentially inert — they won't degrade in wet environments, which makes them ideal for exterior door thresholds, deck posts, and any outdoor leveling job.

**Where it falls short**

Let's be honest about the tradeoffs. Composite shims are harder to trim than wood. A standard utility knife won't slice through them cleanly — you'll need a sharp blade and a firm hand, or a saw for precise cuts. That's a minor annoyance during installation, but it's worth knowing before you start.

The price is also higher than a bundle of wood shims. You're paying for durability and performance, not economy. If you need shims for a single quick project and don't care about longevity, wood shims are cheaper. But if you're a professional or a serious DIYer, the cost difference is justified by the fact that you won't be re-leveling anything later.
