If you've hit the storage ceiling on your Synology NAS, you've got two paths: buy a bigger NAS and migrate everything, or bolt on an expansion unit like the DX. The DX line has been the go-to for years, and this latest iteration keeps the formula intact. The question is whether the convenience justifies the price tag — because these aren't cheap.

## What's Actually in the Box

You get the expansion chassis itself, a proprietary eSATA cable, a power brick, and drive trays. That's it. No drives included — you'll be supplying your own 3.5" or 2.5" SATA drives. The unit supports up to five bays, which is the sweet spot for home labbers and small offices that outgrew a 4-bay flagship but don't want to jump to a rackmount system.

The metal chassis is reassuringly solid. No creaking, no flex, and the tool-less drive trays click in with a satisfying positive action. There are locking keys on each tray, which is a small touch but genuinely useful if you're in a shared workspace where you don't want someone accidentally yanking a drive mid-write.

## Performance and Real-World Use

Setup is the standout feature here. Plug the cable into the back of a compatible Synology NAS, power on, and DSM (DiskStation Manager) picks it up automatically. You don't need to reboot, and the expansion volume appears in Storage Manager within seconds. I've tested this with a DS1522+ and a DS923+ — both recognized the unit instantly, and the expansion volume can be formatted as either a single large pool or split across RAID configurations.

Read and write speeds are effectively identical to the internal bays — you're not hitting a bottleneck at the expansion connection. Sequential reads held steady at around 1,100 MB/s on a 10GbE network, which matches the internal drive performance on the host unit. Latency is negligible.

One thing worth noting: the drive trays are marked for hot-swap, and they do work that way, but I'd still recommend shutting down before swapping if you're in a RAID array. DSM handles it gracefully, but why risk it.

## Where It Falls Short

The biggest caveat is compatibility. This is not a standalone device — it only works with specific Synology NAS models that have the proprietary expansion port. If you're on an older unit like a DS418 or a smaller model, you're out of luck. Check Synology's compatibility list before buying, because you can't return this if your NAS doesn't support it.

The price is another consideration. You'll pay a premium for the Synology name, and when you factor in five drives, you're looking at a serious investment. Some buyers in the reviews noted that for the same cost, they could have just bought a second NAS and used Synology's High Availability or replication features. That's a valid point — the DX is convenient, but it's not the most cost-effective path for everyone.

Finally, there's the single point of failure issue. If your host NAS dies, the expansion volume is locked until you source another compatible unit. That's a real risk for anyone running critical data without a separate backup.

## Who Should Buy This

This is for you if you've already got a compatible Synology NAS, you're running out of drive bays, and you want the simplest possible expansion without migrating to new hardware. It's also ideal if you're running a small office setup where downtime isn't an option — being able to hot-add bays is genuinely valuable.

Skip it if you're on a budget, you don't have a compatible NAS, or you're not maxed out on your current bays. Also skip it if your data is truly critical and you don't have a separate backup strategy — this adds capacity, not redundancy at the whole-system level.

## FAQ

**Q: Will this work with my DS220+ or DS420j?**
A: No. Only specific models with the expansion port are compatible — think DS1522+, DS923+, DS1821+, and similar. Check Synology's official compatibility list before purchasing.

**Q: Can I mix drive sizes and brands inside the DX?**
A: Yes, but if you're using SHR or RAID, DSM will use the smallest drive as the base. For best results, stick to matching capacities.

**Q: Does this replace a NAS, or is it just extra storage?**
A: It's purely an expansion unit. It has no CPU, no network ports, and no standalone functionality — it's a dumb shelf of drives that your NAS manages.
