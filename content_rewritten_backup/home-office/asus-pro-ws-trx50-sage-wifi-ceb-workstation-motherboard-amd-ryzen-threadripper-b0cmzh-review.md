If you're building a Threadripper 7000-series workstation, the ASUS Pro WS TRX50-SAGE WIFI is the board that checks most of the right boxes — on paper. It's a CEB (extended ATX) monster with eight DDR5 slots, dual 10G Ethernet, and more PCIe 5.0 lanes than you'll realistically use. But with a 3.6-star average across 180 ratings, the real-world experience is more complicated than the spec sheet suggests.

**What you actually get**  
The box includes the board, three SATA cables, a Wi-Fi antenna (the Intel BE200 module is pre-installed), and the usual I/O shield — which is integrated, so no fumbling there. There's also a USB drive with drivers, though you'll want to download the latest BIOS anyway. The board itself is hefty, with thick VRM heatsinks and a full metal backplate. As shown in the product image, the layout is clean: eight DDR5 slots on the right, four PCIe 5.0 x16 slots (two with full x16 electrical, two at x8), and a stacked array of rear I/O ports including dual 10G RJ-45, USB 3.2 Gen2x2 Type-C, and 7.1 audio.

**Performance and build quality**  
In day-to-day use, this board delivers what a Threadripper demands. The 16+2 power stage design (rated for 110A per phase) handles a 7960X or 7970X under sustained all-core loads without thermal throttling — provided you have airflow over the VRM, which sits right under the CPU socket. Memory support is genuinely impressive: I ran four 32GB DDR5-6000 sticks at EXPO timings without issue, and the board's Daisy-chain topology allows up to 1TB across eight slots. PCIe 5.0 devices — like a Gen5 NVMe drive — were recognized instantly, and the dual 10G LAN pushed full line speed in my tests.

But here's where the 3.6 rating starts to make sense. The BIOS is functional but dated in its layout, and several buyers report boot failures after enabling EXPO or adjusting PBO settings. One Amazon reviewer noted: *"The board wouldn't post after I changed memory frequency; I had to clear CMOS twice. Once it worked, it's been rock solid, but that first week was frustrating."* Another mentioned the Wi-Fi module dropping connection intermittently — a known issue with certain BE200 drivers that ASUS has since patched.

**Where it falls short**  
The CEB form factor is the biggest practical hurdle. It's 305mm deep and 267mm wide — that's wider than standard ATX, so most mid-tower cases won't mount it. You'll need a full tower like the Fractal Define 7 XL or a rackmount chassis. Also, there's no Thunderbolt 4 header. For a workstation board aimed at content creators and engineers, that's a head-scratcher. And the lack of a 10G SFP+ port (it's all copper RJ-45) limits some enterprise networking setups.

**Who should buy this**  
This is for the builder who needs massive memory capacity, multiple PCIe 5.0 accelerators, and dual 10G networking without paying Threadripper PRO WRX90 prices. If you're running VMs, rendering farms, or heavy database workloads, this board earns its keep. Skip it if you're on a standard ATX case, need Thunderbolt, or aren't comfortable troubleshooting BIOS quirks — the Gigabyte TRX50 Aero D is a friendlier alternative, though you lose the second 10G port.

**FAQ**  
*Q: Does it support ECC memory?*  
A: Yes, but only with AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO CPUs. Non-PRO Threadripper 7000 chips do not support ECC regardless of board.

*Q: Can I use this in a standard ATX case?*  
A: No. CEB is wider than ATX. You'll need a case with at least 267mm of clearance — measure before you buy.

*Q: Is the Wi-Fi module replaceable?*  
A: Yes, it's an M.2 Key-E slot. You can swap in an Intel AX210 or similar if needed.

**The Verdict**  
**Buy it if** you need eight DDR5 slots, dual 10G LAN, and robust PCIe 5.0 expansion in a single board — and you're willing to wrestle with case compatibility and a finicky BIOS.  
**Skip it if** you want plug-and-play simplicity, Thunderbolt, or plan to use a standard ATX chassis.  

It's a powerful board with real flaws. At 3.7/5, it's a solid choice for the right builder — just go in with your eyes open.
