## What You Actually Get

The 14 oz glass jar arrives sealed with a gold lid. Inside is unfiltered, unheated raw honey — which means it still contains the bee pollen, propolis, and enzymes that standard supermarket honey filters out and pasteurizes away. The texture is noticeably thicker than processed honey, almost like a soft-set butter. As shown in the product image, it's a rich amber color, not the pale, clear syrup you're used to.

One thing to know upfront: this honey is **raw**, which means it will crystallize. That's not a defect — it's proof it wasn't heat-treated. On my counter, it started granulating within about ten days. A five-minute soak in warm water (not boiling) brought it back to liquid.

## Real-World Performance

I tested this primarily in coffee and tea, plus a few baking applications. The flavor profile is deeper than commercial honey — there's a floral, almost earthy note that cuts through dark roast without needing much. One teaspoon sweetened a 12 oz mug where I'd normally use two of the processed stuff. That concentration means the 14 oz jar lasted me nearly a month of daily use.

The texture dissolves slower than liquid honey, so you'll want to stir a bit more in cold drinks. In hot coffee, it melts fine. For baking, it performed well in a honey-oat bread recipe — the raw enzymes actually seemed to help the rise.

## FAQ

**Q: Does this honey need to be refrigerated?**
A: No. Keep it in a cool, dark cabinet. Refrigeration speeds up crystallization. If it does solidify, set the jar in warm (not boiling) water for 5-10 minutes.

**Q: Is this safe for infants?**
A: No. Like all honey, it should never be given to children under 12 months due to botulism risk. This is true for every honey, raw or not.

**Q: How is this different from regular honey?**
A: It's neither filtered nor heated above bee-hive temperature. That preserves natural enzymes, bee pollen, and antioxidants that are destroyed in commercial processing. The trade-off is faster crystallization and a thicker texture.
