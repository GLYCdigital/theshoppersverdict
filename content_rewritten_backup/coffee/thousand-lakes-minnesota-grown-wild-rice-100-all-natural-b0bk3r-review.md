## Performance and Real-World Cooking

Here's where Thousand Lakes earns its keep. The grains are remarkably uniform in size, which matters more than you'd think. Cheaper wild rice blends have a frustrating mix of short, broken grains that overcook into mush alongside longer grains that stay crunchy. This batch cooked evenly — I tested it three ways:

**The classic simmer:** 1 cup rice to 3 cups salted water, simmered covered for 50 minutes. Result: grains split open, showing that creamy interior, with a firm bite that isn't chalky or hard. The broth took on a deep, almost tea-like color.

**The pilaf route:** Toasting the dry grains in butter for 3 minutes before adding liquid deepened the nutty notes significantly. This is where the lake-harvested character shines — there's a subtle sweetness and earthy complexity that cultivated rice just doesn't develop.

**The leftover test:** Cooked rice held up for 5 days in the fridge without turning to paste. Reheated beautifully in a skillet with a splash of broth. That's a mark of quality grain.

## FAQ: Three Questions Shoppers Actually Ask

**Q: How does this compare to paddy-grown wild rice?**
A: Night and day. Lake-harvested rice has deeper flavor, firmer texture, and darker color. Paddy rice is milder, softer, and cheaper — but it's a different product.

**Q: Is this really 100% wild rice, or is it a blend?**
A: Confirmed — it's pure wild rice (Zizania palustris), not a blend with white or brown rice. If you see mixed grains, that's not this product.

**Q: How should I cook it for best results?**
A: Rinse thoroughly, use a 1:3 rice-to-liquid ratio, simmer covered for 50-60 minutes. Don't peek. Rest 10 minutes before fluffing. For soup, cook separately and add at the end so it doesn't overcook.
