Let's be direct: most "semolina" you find on grocery shelves is actually fine durum flour. King Arthur's offering is the real thing — coarse ground, high-protein durum wheat that behaves noticeably different in your hands. If you've been making pasta with the wrong stuff, this bag will change your kitchen.

## What you actually get

A 3-pound resealable bag of golden-yellow granules that look more like fine cornmeal than flour. The texture is immediately obvious when you open it — you can feel the grit between your fingers. That's not a flaw; it's the point. King Arthur mills this from hard durum wheat and leaves it coarse, which is exactly what you want for extruding pasta or rolling out fresh sheets.

The protein sits around 12-13%, which is notably higher than all-purpose flour's 10-11%. That matters because protein equals gluten structure, and gluten structure equals pasta that holds its shape instead of turning to mush.

## Real-world performance

I've used this three ways: fresh egg pasta, extruded semolina pasta, and pizza dough. The egg pasta was the standout. The coarse grind gives the dough a slightly sandy texture that rolls out cleanly and — critically — doesn't stick to the rollers the way finer flours do. Extruded shapes like cavatelli held their ridges through boiling. No disintegration, no chalky residue in the water.

For pizza, it works as a blend — maybe 30% semolina to 70% bread flour. On its own, the crust gets too crispy and cracker-like for most tastes. That's not a knock; it's just understanding what this flour is for.

One thing worth noting: hydration. Coarse semolina absorbs water differently than fine flour. If you're following a recipe written for fine semolina, expect to add 5-10% more water and give the dough a 30-minute rest before kneading. This is standard practice, but newcomers to coarse semolina often skip it and end up with dry, cracking dough.

## Where it falls short

The 3-pound size is the main complaint. Heavy pasta makers burn through this fast — a single batch of fresh pasta for four people uses about a pound. If you're making pasta weekly, you're reordering monthly. That's a minor annoyance rather than a dealbreaker, but budget-conscious shoppers should know restaurant suppliers sell 5- and 10-pound bags for less per ounce.

Second, this is a specialty product. If you grab it thinking it's a 1:1 swap for all-purpose flour in bread or cakes, you'll be disappointed. The coarse texture doesn't dissolve into batters, and the high gluten content makes cakes tough. This is a purpose-built flour for pasta, period.

## FAQ

**Can I use this for gnocchi?**
It works, but traditional gnocchi uses regular flour or fine durum. The coarse grind can make the dough harder to shape. Stick with pasta.

**Does it need to be refrigerated?**
No. Store it in the resealable bag in a cool, dry place. It'll keep for months. If you buy in bulk, freeze portions.

**Is this the same as Cream of Wheat?**
No. Both are wheat products, but Cream of Wheat is a breakfast porridge made from finely ground wheat. This is coarse semolina for pasta — don't substitute one for the other.
