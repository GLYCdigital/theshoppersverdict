Most wheat on grocery shelves is a chemical soup you never asked for. Glyphosate — the active ingredient in Roundup — is routinely sprayed on conventional wheat as a desiccant before harvest. Palouse Brand is betting that enough shoppers care about that fact to pay a premium for grain that's actually been tested, not just marketed as clean.

Here's what makes this different: Palouse Brand doesn't just claim glyphosate-free. They send samples to independent labs and publish the results. That's a meaningful distinction in a category full of vague "clean food" marketing. At 4.7 stars across 235 ratings, buyers seem to agree.

**What you're actually getting**

This is a 25-pound bag of whole soft white wheat berries — not flour. The kernels are grown on family farms in Washington's Palouse region, certified organic, and verified glyphosate residue-free through third-party testing. As shown in the product image, you're getting whole grain in its most unprocessed form.

You'll need a grain mill, a high-powered blender, or a wheat grinder attachment for your stand mixer to actually use these. That's an upfront investment — anywhere from $50 for a hand-crank mill to $300+ for electric options. Factor that into your cost calculation before you buy.

**Real-world performance**

Soft white wheat is the baker's workhorse for anything tender. It has lower protein than hard red wheat, which sounds like a downside but is actually ideal for pancakes, muffins, cakes, cookies, and pastries. Fresh-milled flour from these berries produces noticeably sweeter, lighter baked goods than commercial all-purpose flour.

One Amazon reviewer put it bluntly: "I've been milling my own wheat for 15 years and this is the cleanest, sweetest soft white I've found. The difference in my pancakes is night and day."

Another long-term buyer noted the consistency: "Third bag I've ordered. Same quality every time. The lab results they post online give me peace of mind my kids aren't eating Roundup."

**Where it falls short**

Let's be direct about the tradeoffs. First, if you're expecting to make crusty artisan sourdough loaves, this is the wrong wheat. Soft white's low protein means weak gluten development — your bread will be tender and cake-like, not chewy. You want hard red wheat for that.

Second, the price. This costs roughly double what conventional organic wheat berries run. You're paying for the testing, the certification, and the family-farm sourcing. If glyphosate exposure doesn't concern you, this premium won't make sense.

Third, storage is on you. Whole berries last significantly longer than flour — up to a decade in sealed containers with oxygen absorbers — but you need to think about bulk storage. A 25-pound bag demands proper containers and a plan.
