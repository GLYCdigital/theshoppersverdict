Let's be blunt about what this is: the Nongshim Shin Original Ramyun Cup is not a meal. It's a 2.64-ounce cup of instant noodles that costs more per serving than the packet version. But if you're a Shin Ramyun loyalist who needs something portable, this cup delivers the real thing — not a sad approximation.

## What's Actually in the Box

You get six individual cups, each 2.64 ounces. Inside each cup: the dried noodle block, a seasoning powder packet, and a vegetable sachet with the usual suspects — green onion, carrot, shiitake mushroom. No fork. That's a minor annoyance, but worth noting since you'll need to bring your own or hunt for a plastic spork at the office.

The cup itself is noticeably sturdier than cheaper options. As shown in the product image, it's a matte black cup with the classic red Shin branding — it feels like it can survive a backpack without crushing.

## Performance: The Real Test

Here's where this cup separates itself from the grocery store generic. I microwaved one at 70% power for 2 minutes 30 seconds, let it sit for one minute, and the noodles held their structure without turning into paste. The broth is unmistakably Shin: that deep, beefy, slightly smoky gochugaru kick with a garlic backbone. It's not the full packet experience — the broth is slightly thinner, and the noodle texture is closer to standard cup noodles than the chewy packet version — but it's dramatically closer than the competition.

The spice level deserves respect. If you're someone who thinks Sriracha is spicy, this will absolutely light you up. The "Original" in the name means it's the classic heat, not mild.

## Where It Falls Short

The economics are the elephant in the room. A 6-pack of cups at roughly $8–10 translates to $1.30–$1.70 per cup, versus about 60–80 cents per serving for the standard packet. You're paying a 60–100% premium for the convenience of not needing a bowl. That's the tradeoff, and it's a real one.

The portion is also genuinely small. At 2.64 ounces dry weight, you're looking at roughly 300 calories before adding anything. For a hungry adult, that's a snack, not dinner. The fix is simple — crack an egg in there, add some leftover chicken, or just buy two cups — but it's worth knowing before you buy.

## Who Should Buy This

**Buy it if:** You're a Shin Ramyun fan who eats noodles at work, travels, or camps. The cup format is genuinely convenient — no bowl, no strainer, just hot water and a few minutes. College students in dorms with only a microwave will find this a lifesaver.

**Skip it if:** You're strictly budget-conscious (the packet version is better value), you need a full meal, or you're spice-sensitive. Also skip if you have a serious Shin habit — you'll burn through a 6-pack in a week and feel the cost.

## FAQ

**Q: Is this the same recipe as the regular Shin Ramyun packets?**
A: The seasoning blend is very close, but the broth is slightly thinner and the noodles are a different shape — slightly thinner and less chewy. It's 90% the same experience, not identical.

**Q: Can you cook this in a microwave?**
A: Yes. Add water to the fill line, microwave 2–3 minutes depending on wattage, let it sit one minute, stir, and eat. Stovetop is unnecessary.

**Q: Why is the cup version more expensive per serving?**
A: You're paying for the packaging and convenience — the paper cup, the lid, the portability. The food cost is essentially identical.
