Let's get one thing straight: arrowroot powder is not a 1:1 flour substitute for bread. It doesn't have gluten's elasticity, and if you treat it like wheat flour, you'll end up with gummy, dense bricks. But for thickening sauces, crisping up gluten-free baked goods, and making chewy cookies that don't crumble on the first bite, it's indispensable. Anthony's Organic version is the one I keep coming back to.

**What you actually get**

The 4 lb bag is substantial — roughly twice the size of the typical Bob's Red Mill jar. The powder inside is fine, silky, and pure white, with a neutral smell. You're paying for the convenience of a bulk size, but you're also paying for the testing. Anthony's posts batch-specific gluten test results online, which matters if you're genuinely celiac, not just gluten-sensitive. The bag itself is resealable with a zipper top, though I'd recommend transferring to a glass jar for long-term storage — the zipper works fine for a few weeks but can wear out with heavy use.

**How it performs in real cooking**

I tested this three ways: a classic pan sauce, gluten-free pancakes, and a fruit pie filling. The pan sauce was the star — a tablespoon whisked into cold stock dissolved instantly with zero lumps, and it thickened to a glossy finish without turning cloudy. Pancakes got a noticeably crisper edge and a tender crumb, though they needed an extra egg for structure. Pie filling set beautifully, but here's the tradeoff: arrowroot-thickened fillings can turn slightly slimy if you overheat them or let them sit too long. Keep heat moderate and serve promptly.

One thing I noticed: arrowroot is more forgiving than cornstarch in acidic liquids. If you're thickening a tomato-based sauce or something with citrus, this won't break down the way cornstarch can. That's a real advantage.

**Where it falls short**

Honestly, the product itself is solid. The downsides are practical: no scoop included, so you'll need your own measuring spoons. And the 4 lb size is a commitment — if you only use arrowroot occasionally, it can sit in your pantry for a year or more. Arrowroot doesn't spoil quickly, but it can absorb moisture if stored poorly, which ruins the texture. Also, if you're expecting a direct wheat flour replacement for yeast breads, you'll be disappointed. It's a thickener and a texture enhancer, not a standalone flour.

**FAQ**

**Q: Can I substitute arrowroot flour 1:1 for cornstarch?**
A: Yes, but adjust liquid amounts slightly. Arrowroot thickens at a lower temperature and can thin out if boiled too long. Use about 2 teaspoons per cup of liquid instead of the standard tablespoon of cornstarch.

**Q: Does this need to be cooked before eating?**
A: Yes. Unlike some starches, raw arrowroot has an unpleasant chalky texture. It needs heat to activate its thickening properties, so always incorporate it into cooked dishes.

**Q: How long does the 4 lb bag last?**
A: Stored in a cool, dry place (or a sealed jar), it stays good for up to two years. I'd say a typical household gets 6–12 months of regular use from this size.
