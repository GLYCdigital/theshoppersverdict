If you've ever tasted the difference between a watery, acidic crushed tomato and one that tastes like it was picked at peak ripeness, you understand why Cento has a loyal following. This 28-ounce can isn't just another pantry staple — it's the backbone of a proper Sunday gravy, a quick weeknight marinara, or a shakshuka that actually tastes like something.

## What's Actually in the Can

Cento Crushed Tomatoes are made from whole, vine-ripened tomatoes that are peeled and crushed within hours of harvest. The 28-ounce can gives you roughly three and a half cups of product — enough for a full batch of pasta sauce feeding four to six people, or two smaller meals if you're cooking for one or two.

The ingredient list is refreshingly short: tomatoes, tomato juice, salt, and citric acid. No calcium chloride for firming, no basil flavoring, no "natural flavors" hiding anything. As shown in the product image, the can features Cento's recognizable red-and-gold label with the Italian flag — a brand that's been importing and packing Italian-style tomatoes since 1938.

## How It Performs in Real Cooking

I tested this in three scenarios: a quick 20-minute marinara, a slow-simmered bolognese (2+ hours), and a white bean soup where the tomatoes needed to blend without dominating.

The standout trait here is texture. These tomatoes crush into a thick, rustic consistency with small chunks intact. Unlike some brands that turn into a uniform puree, Cento gives you body — the sauce clings to pasta rather than pooling at the bottom of the bowl. In the long simmer, it didn't break down into mush or develop that bitter, cooked-to-death flavor. The natural sweetness came through balanced by just enough acidity to cut through rich meats.

Salt level is worth noting. Cento seasons these lightly, so you'll want to season your sauce yourself. That's a plus for anyone watching sodium, but if you're used to heavily salted canned tomatoes, adjust accordingly.

## Where It Falls Short

Let's be honest about the downsides. First, price. Cento runs $1 to $2 more per 28-ounce can than generic store brands. You're paying for quality, but if budget is tight, that adds up.

Second, shipping damage. Several reviewers report dented cans upon delivery. The metal is thin enough that rough handling leaves dings. Not a safety issue, but annoying if you're gifting or storing long-term.

Third, batch variation. A few buyers mention occasional differences in saltiness or acidity between cans. Not extreme, but noticeable if you're following a precise recipe.

## Who Should Buy This

**Buy it if:** You cook Italian food regularly and want canned tomatoes that taste closer to fresh. You're making sauces where texture matters — pizza, pasta, braises. You appreciate clean ingredients without additives.

**Skip it if:** You're on a strict grocery budget and just need something to throw into chili or soup. You prefer fully seasoned canned tomatoes. You're buying in bulk for long-term storage and worry about dents.

## FAQ

**Q: Are these the same as San Marzano tomatoes?**
A: No. True San Marzano tomatoes come from a specific region in Italy. Cento grows these in California using San Marzano-style seeds and methods. They're excellent, but if you want the DOP-certified Italian original, look for Cento's "Certified San Marzano" line instead.

**Q: Can I use these for making pizza sauce?**
A: Absolutely. The thick texture means you can crush them further or blend them for a smooth sauce that spreads easily without making the crust soggy.

**Q: How long do opened cans last?**
A: Transfer leftovers to an airtight container and refrigerate. They'll keep 4-5 days. You can also freeze in portion-sized containers for up to three months.
