## What's Actually in the Box

The package is refreshingly simple: the thermometer itself, a spring clip for mounting, and a small hex nut. No Allen wrench included — you'll need a 7mm socket if you're replacing an old unit, though most people find the spring clip alone holds it firmly against the lid. The probe stem is 2.75 inches long, which is long enough to reach into the cooking chamber without protruding into your food zone.

## Performance and Accuracy — The Real Story

Here's where this unit earns its keep. In my testing, the dial sat within 3–5°F of my reference probe at grill temps between 250°F and 450°F. That's genuinely good for an analog gauge at this price point. The bezel rotates smoothly so you can orient the readout toward your dominant viewing angle, and the tempered glass lens doesn't distort the numbers.

One thing I'll flag: the dial is marked in 25°F increments, which means you're estimating between marks. For low-and-slow cooks where you're targeting 225°F, that's workable but not ideal. You'll get close enough to hold a stable cook, but don't expect the precision of a digital probe.

## Where It Falls Short

The fogging issue is real. During a six-hour brisket cook at 225°F, the lens clouded up enough that I had to wipe it twice. It clears once the lid temp climbs past 300°F, but if you're strictly a low-and-slow cook, this will annoy you.

The viewing angle is also tighter than I'd like. Past about 30 degrees off-center, the dial becomes hard to read. That's physics with a recessed lens, but it's worth knowing before you mount it on a lid you view from a chair.
