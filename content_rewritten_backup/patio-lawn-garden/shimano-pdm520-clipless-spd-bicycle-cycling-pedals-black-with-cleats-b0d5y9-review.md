If you're shopping for your first pair of clipless pedals or replacing a worn-out set on a commuter or trail bike, the Shimano PDM520 is the benchmark. It's not flashy, not carbon-fiber light, and certainly not Instagram-worthy — but it does exactly what hundreds of thousands of riders need without drama.

**What's in the box**

Shimano keeps it simple: the pedals, a pair of SM-SH51 single-release cleats, and the mounting hardware. That's it. No fancy packaging, no torque wrench — just a 6mm Allen key and about 10 minutes of your time. The cleats alone retail for around $15–20, which makes the $62 price tag more palatable. As one buyer put it: "Came with the cleats included. That's a $20 value right there. Install took me five minutes."

**Build and real-world performance**

The PDM520 is essentially the successor to the legendary M520, and Shimano's engineers made smart tweaks. The retention mechanism sits recessed inside a reinforced plastic cage, which protects the binding when you bash a rock. The axle uses a chromoly spindle with a sealed cartridge bearing system — the same internals found in pedals costing twice as much. In practice, this means smooth rotation from day one and minimal maintenance for years.

Dual-sided entry is the killer feature here. On the older single-sided pedals, you'd fumble to flip the pedal with your toe before clipping in at a stoplight. The 520s eliminate that dance entirely. Just stomp — you're in. For commuters navigating city traffic or trail riders who dab a foot on technical climbs, this is genuinely transformative.

The cage also sheds mud better than the older design. Shimano opened up the area around the retention mechanism, so trail grime has fewer places to pack in and jam the release. That said, the plastic cage is a tradeoff — it's tough enough for normal use, but a direct hit on a sharp rock can crack it. The binding still works, but you'll want to replace the pedal body eventually.

**What riders consistently praise**

The 4.7-star rating across 235 reviews isn't an accident. The dominant theme in customer feedback is reliability. "Had these for two years of daily commuting in rain and snow. Never needed to service them, still click in perfectly," one reviewer notes. Another echoes the value sentiment: "These are 90% of the performance of pedals three times the price."

The SM-SH51 cleats that ship in the box are also worth mentioning. They're the "single release" version, meaning you twist your heel outward to unclip — the standard SPD behavior. If you want multi-directional release, you'd swap to SM-SH56 cleats, but the included ones are perfect for 95% of riders.

**Where it falls short**

Honesty time. At 380 grams per pair, these aren't light. If you're building a sub-15-pound race bike, look at the XT or XTR line. The plastic cage is the other compromise — it does its job protecting the mechanism, but it flexes slightly under hard pedaling. Most riders won't notice, but power sprinters might.

The tension adjustment is also fiddly. Each pedal has a small screw that requires a 3mm hex wrench, and the range of adjustment feels subtle. You'll likely set it once and never touch it again, but dialing it in initially takes a few test rides.

**Who should buy these**

- **Commuter cyclists** who clip in and out frequently at traffic lights
- **Entry-level mountain bikers** wanting reliable, forgiving SPD engagement
- **Touring and gravel riders** who prioritize durability over weight
- **Anyone replacing worn-out pedals** on a budget without sacrificing quality

Skip them if you're a weight-obsessed road racer or demand metal-bodied pedals for aggressive downhill riding.

**FAQ**

**Q: Will these work with any SPD-compatible shoe?**
A: Yes. The two-bolt cleat pattern is standard across all SPD shoes — from $60 commuter shoes to $300 mountain bike shoes.

**Q: Are the cleats included?**
A: Yes, SM-SH51 cleats and mounting hardware ship in the box. This is a $15–20 value versus buying them separately.

**Q: How hard is it to clip in for a beginner?**
A: The dual-sided design makes it significantly easier than single-sided pedals. Start with the tension screw loosened, practice in a doorway, and you'll be comfortable within a week.

**The Verdict**

**Buy it if** you want reliable, dual-sided SPD performance at a price that doesn't punish you for trying clipless pedals or replacing tired ones. The included cleats, proven Shimano internals, and mud-shedding cage make this the smartest $62 you'll spend on your bike this year.

**Skip it if** you're chasing grams for racing or need a bombproof metal body for aggressive trail riding.
