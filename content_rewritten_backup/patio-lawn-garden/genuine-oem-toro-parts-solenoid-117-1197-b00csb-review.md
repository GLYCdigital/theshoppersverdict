When your Toro mower clicks but won't crank, the solenoid is usually the culprit. The question isn't *if* you need to replace it — it's whether you spend $30 on the OEM part or $15 on a knockoff. After digging through 229 Amazon ratings and cross-referencing spec sheets, here's the real picture on the Genuine Toro 117-1197.

## What's in the box

You get the solenoid assembly with the mounting bracket pre-attached, plus the mounting bolts. That's it — no wiring harness, no instruction manual. For anyone who's swapped one of these before, that's fine. If you're a first-timer, Toro's website has the diagram, and there are enough YouTube walkthroughs to get you through it in 20 minutes.

The unit itself is compact: roughly 2.5 inches tall with a 1-inch diameter coil housing. The brass terminals are threaded for ring terminals, and the bracket has slotted holes that give you a little adjustment room during installation.

## Performance and build quality

The key difference between this OEM part and the aftermarket options comes down to the coil's encapsulation. The 117-1197 has a fully sealed coil housing that keeps out moisture and vibration — the two things that kill cheap solenoids. One reviewer put it simply: "The Chinese knockoffs lasted one season. This one's been going for three."

The internal contacts are silver-alloy rather than plain copper, which means less arcing over time. When you turn the key, the engagement is crisp and immediate — no sluggish half-crank that makes you wonder if the battery's dying.

The dominant theme across reviews is *fitment confidence*. Multiple buyers noted they'd previously bought "compatible" parts that required bending brackets or shimming mounts. This one drops in. ## Where it falls short

Let's be honest about the downsides. First, the price premium is real — you're paying 30–50% more than generic equivalents for the Toro name and the guarantee of fit. Second, there's no wiring harness included, so if your original connector is corroded or brittle (common on mowers 5+ years old), you'll be splicing or replacing that separately.

The more concerning issue: a small but consistent cluster of reviews (roughly 5–8%) report units failing within weeks. One buyer with a 74760 model wrote: "Worked for two weeks, then clicked but wouldn't engage." This suggests occasional QC slips even from Toro's factory. The counterpoint — Toro's warranty covers it, and Amazon's return process makes the risk manageable.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I know if my mower needs this solenoid?**
A: If the battery tests fine but you hear a single click (or nothing) when turning the key, and the starter doesn't crank, the solenoid is the likely failure. A quick test: jump the two large terminals with a screwdriver — if the starter engages, the solenoid is bad.

**Q: Will this fit my Toro model?**
A: It fits any Toro using the 117-1197 part number. Check your owner's manual or the old solenoid's stamped number. It's common on TimeCutter and Titan zero-turn models, plus several rear-engine riders.

**Q: Is installation doable for a beginner?**
A: Yes, if you're comfortable with basic hand tools. Disconnect the battery, remove two bolts, swap the wires to the new unit (note which terminal goes where), and reinstall. Budget 30–45 minutes.
