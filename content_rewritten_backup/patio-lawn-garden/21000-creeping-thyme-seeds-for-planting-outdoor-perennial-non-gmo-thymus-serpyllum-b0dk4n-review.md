**The 21,000-seed gamble: volume over reliability**

You're staring at a pouch claiming 21,000 creeping thyme seeds for less than the cost of a coffee. Tempting. But here's the reality: this is a numbers game, and the house doesn't always win. With a 3.2-star average across 160 ratings, buyers are split between "lush purple carpet" and "mostly empty dirt." I've dug through the feedback, the growing specs, and the fine print to tell you where this lands.

**What's actually in the packet**

You get one foil pouch — no fancy packaging, no growing guide worth reading. The seeds are Thymus serpyllum, the true creeping thyme, not a knockoff blend. They're impossibly small, like dark dust. That's a problem I'll get to. Expect roughly 0.5 grams of seed total, which sounds pathetic but is normal for thyme — 21,000 seeds is a real count, not marketing inflation.

**The germination lottery**

Here's the honest truth: creeping thyme is a slow, fussy germinator, and this batch doesn't help itself. Multiple buyer reviews describe identical scenarios — scattering on prepared soil, keeping it moist, waiting three weeks, then seeing only sparse sprouts. One reviewer wrote, "Followed every instruction, kept the soil damp, and got maybe 30 plants out of the whole packet." Another countered, "Planted in late spring, had a carpet by August." The difference? Soil temperature and stratification.

Creeping thyme needs cold stratification (30-60 days in the fridge) or a warm soil temp above 65°F to break dormancy reliably. This product's instructions skip that entirely. If you plant in spring without cold treatment, you're gambling on a 20-30% germination rate. That's not a defective product — it's a missing instruction sheet.

**Where it shines**

When seeds do take, the plants are vigorous. True creeping thyme spreads via rooting stems, not just seeds, so established patches fill in aggressively. Buyers report it working beautifully between stepping stones, along walkways, and as a lawn alternative in dry, sunny spots. It's drought-tolerant, releases that classic herbal scent when walked on, and produces clusters of tiny purple blooms that pollinators adore.

The value math also works in your favor if you're patient. Even at a 20% germination rate, you're looking at 4,000+ viable plants from one packet. That's still a fraction of the cost of nursery flats, which run $3-5 per 2-inch pot.

**The honest downsides**

Sowing is the first hurdle. The seeds are so fine they clump and scatter unevenly. Mix with fine sand (1:10 ratio) before broadcasting — the instructions don't tell you this, but it's essential. Expect bald patches where seeds clumped and drowned or blew away.

Germination time is another test of patience. Even under ideal conditions, expect 14-28 days to first sprouts, and 8-12 weeks before you see meaningful coverage. This is not a "plant and forget" ground cover. It needs consistent moisture for the first month, which means daily watering in most climates.

Finally, the seed-to-plant timeline. Full coverage of a 100 sq ft area? That's a 6-8 month project, not a single season. Creeping thyme is slow to establish from seed compared to plugs or sod.

**Who should buy this**

- **The patient gardener**: You're okay with a 2-year timeline to a full thyme lawn and enjoy the process.
- **The budget landscaper**: Covering a large area on pennies per square foot, accepting partial results in year one.
- **The experimenter**: You want to try creeping thyme without committing to expensive nursery plants.

**Skip it if** you need quick ground cover this season, you're not willing to stratify seeds in the fridge, or you lack the discipline for daily watering. Buy plugs instead — you'll pay more but get guaranteed plants.

**FAQ**

**Q: How long does it take to see sprouts?**
With cold stratification and 65-70°F soil, expect 14-21 days. Without stratification, some seeds may take 30-45 days or never sprout.

**Q: Will this really cover 100 square feet?**
Theoretically, yes — at 200 seeds per square foot. In practice, expect 30-50% coverage in year one, with full coverage by year two as plants spread.

**Q: Can I plant this in fall?**
Yes, and fall planting actually helps — natural cold stratification over winter improves spring germination. Just sow before the ground freezes.
