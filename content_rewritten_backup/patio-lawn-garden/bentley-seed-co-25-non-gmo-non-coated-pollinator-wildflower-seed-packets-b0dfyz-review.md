## What's in the box

You get 25 individual 1-gram packets, each containing a distinct species or blend — think cosmos, zinnia, bachelor's buttons, calendula, black-eyed Susan, and several others. Each packet is labeled with the variety name, so you're not playing seed roulette. The box itself is sturdy enough to double as storage, though the packets are thin foil-laminated paper that hold up fine as long as you don't crush them.

The "non-coated" part is worth emphasizing. Many mass-market wildflower mixes coat seeds with clay or polymer to make them easier to sow mechanically. That coating can delay germination or inhibit it in dry conditions. Plain, uncoated seed — what you get here — needs consistent moisture but responds faster when conditions are right.

## Where it falls short

Let's be honest about the tradeoffs. These are 1-gram packets — that's roughly a teaspoon of seed. For a large meadow or a 100-square-foot patch, you'll need multiple boxes. That's by design (it's a sampler), but if your goal is maximum coverage per dollar, you're better off with a bulk pound bag from a regional seed house.

Germination rates vary by species, which is true of any uncoated mix. A few reviewers reported spotty results with specific varieties — one flagged the poppy packet as slow. That's not a defect; poppies are notoriously finicky about soil disturbance and light. But it's worth knowing going in.

The packaging, while functional, isn't resealable. If you don't use a full packet, fold the top over and clip it, or you'll risk moisture damage.

## FAQ

**Q: Are these actually non-GMO?**
A: Yes. The seeds are open-pollinated varieties, not hybrids, and the company doesn't use GMO stock. That's verified in the product documentation, not just marketing language.

**Q: When should I sow them?**
A: Most of these are warm-season annuals. Wait until after your last frost date. In zones 3-6, that's typically May; in zones 7-9, April is fine. Some, like the poppies, prefer fall sowing in warmer zones.

**Q: Will they grow in partial shade?**
A: Most of these species prefer full sun (6+ hours). In partial shade, expect reduced bloom counts, not total failure. The calendula and bachelor's buttons handle a bit less sun than the rest.
