The box contains the salt cell itself, pre-installed flow sensor, and the necessary O-rings for installation. **The Real-World Performance Problem**

Here's where things get uncomfortable. The fundamental issue isn't the design — it's the execution. Multiple verified buyers report the same pattern: the cell works initially, generating chlorine at acceptable rates, then degrades rapidly. As one reviewer put it: *"Worked great for 2 months, then output dropped to almost nothing. Checked the plates and they looked coated in scale that wouldn't clean off."*

That's a recurring theme in the negative feedback — premature scaling and electrode degradation that cleaning cycles can't fix. Another buyer noted: *"Installed in April, by July the system was throwing the low-salt error constantly even though salt levels were fine. Pentair cell lasted 5 years. This lasted 5 months."*

**The Numbers Don't Lie**

The 2.0 rating isn't a fluke. Roughly 60% of the 150 ratings are 1-star, and the distribution shows a clear bimodal pattern: a meaningful minority (about 25%) report satisfactory performance, while the majority experience premature failure. What's striking is that the positive reviews typically come from users in warmer climates with year-round pools, while failures cluster in seasonal-use scenarios — suggesting the cell may not handle the rest/shock cycles as well as OEM.

**The Honest Buying Advice**

If your IC40 needs replacing and you're handy — meaning you're comfortable disassembling, inspecting, and cleaning cells regularly — the Kolasina *might* work for you, especially if you're willing to treat it as a 12-18 month consumable rather than a 3-5 year investment. At roughly $350-400, that's a break-even proposition if you're handy with a multimeter and can verify actual chlorine output weekly.

If you want set-and-forget reliability, or your pool chemistry varies (high calcium, heavy bather load, frequent rain dilution), skip it. A dead cell mid-season means no sanitizer, green water, and a rushed emergency purchase — which is how people end up paying OEM prices anyway.
