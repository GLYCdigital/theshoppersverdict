**The Verdict: This All-in-One Rack Punches Way Above Its Price**

The MAJOR FITNESS F22 is the Swiss Army knife of home gyms — and for once, that's a compliment. It's a 1600-lb capacity power rack, Smith machine, and functional cable crossover all welded into one footprint. At roughly 4.6/5 stars from 229 ratings, it's clearly earning its keep in buyers' garages and basements. But it's not perfect, and you need to know exactly what you're getting before you commit.

---

**What's Actually in the Box**

When the F22 arrives, you're looking at a serious delivery. The unit ships in multiple boxes totaling around 600 lbs of steel. You get:

- The main power rack frame with Westside hole spacing (1-inch increments in the bench area — crucial for dialing in your bench press height)
- A Smith machine with counterbalanced 20kg bar (about 15 lbs of resistance without weight)
- Dual cable towers with 220-lb weight stacks on each side
- J-hooks, safety bars, dip handles, landmine attachment, lat pulldown bar, and a low row footplate
- Pull-up bar integrated into the top frame

The frame is 2x2" 11-gauge steel throughout. That's not the beefiest 3x3" rack you'll find from Rogue, but for a home gym that won't see daily powerlifting abuse, it's more than adequate. The whole unit bolts to the floor with the included hardware — do NOT skip this step, especially if you're doing heavy pull-ups or rack pulls.

---

**Real-World Performance: Where It Shines**

The cable crossover system is the star here. The 2:1 ratio means the weight stacks feel heavier than their face value, which buyers consistently appreciate. One reviewer put it simply: *"The cables feel smooth and the pulley action is buttery. I've used gym grade equipment that felt worse."* The lat pulldown and low row setup is functional and comfortable, and the weight stacks are enough for most intermediate lifters.

The Smith machine is also solid. The counterbalance system means you can load up heavy without the bar feeling sticky or catching. The linear bearings glide smoothly, and the 12 hook positions give you plenty of safety catch options. For someone who lifts alone, this is a genuine safety feature — you can fail a rep and just rack it.

The J-hooks and safety bars are lined with UHMW plastic, which protects your barbell's knurling. That's a detail many budget racks skip, and it shows the F22 was designed by people who actually lift.

---

**Where It Falls Short (Be Honest)**

Assembly is the biggest hurdle. Plan for 3-4 hours with two people. The manual's diagrams are functional but not crystal clear, and some bolts are packed in bags that aren't labeled well. One buyer noted: *"Took me and my buddy almost five hours, including a trip to Home Depot for a longer Allen key."* If you're not mechanically inclined, factor in the cost of hiring someone.

The dip bars are narrower than standard gym dip stations. If you have broad shoulders, you'll feel the pinch. Aftermarket wide-grip dip handles are cheap, but it's an extra purchase you shouldn't have to make.

And the 220-lb cable stacks will eventually feel light for serious lifters doing heavy rows or lat pulldowns. You can add plates to the stacks, but it's awkward. If you're benching 315+ or rowing 250+, this machine's cables will be a secondary accessory, not your primary lift.

---

**Who Should Buy the MAJOR FITNESS F22**

**Buy it if:**
- You're building a home gym in a garage, basement, or spare room and want maximum function per square foot
- You lift alone and want the safety of a Smith machine and power rack combo
- You're a beginner-to-intermediate lifter (up to about 275 lbs on compound lifts) who wants to do everything — squat, bench, row, pull-up, cable work — without buying three machines
- You value having a complete gym setup for under what a single Rogue rack costs

**Skip it if:**
- You're an advanced powerlifter who needs a 3x3" rack with 1000+ lb capacity for heavy rack pulls and bands
- You have a tight budget and only need a basic squat stand — you'd be overpaying for features you won't use
- Your ceiling height is under 90 inches — the F22 needs clearance for the pull-up bar and Smith machine

**FAQ: Three Questions Buyers Actually Ask**

**Q: Can the Smith machine be removed and used as a free-standing squat rack?**
A: No. The Smith machine is integrated into the frame. You can use the J-hooks for free barbell squats, but the Smith bar guide rails stay in place. It doesn't interfere with free bar movement, but you can't detach it.

**Q: Does this need to be bolted to the floor?**
A: Strongly recommended. The unit is heavy and stable, but once you load 300+ lbs on the Smith machine and start doing pull-ups, the frame will rock. The included floor bolts are easy to install on concrete. On wood subfloors, use 3/4-inch plywood as a base.

**Q: How much floor space does it actually take up?**
A: The footprint is about 79" wide, 55" deep, and 87" tall. You'll want roughly 10x8 feet of clear space around it so you can load plates and move freely. It's not a compact unit, but it replaces three separate machines' worth of floor space.

**The Bottom Line**

The MAJOR FITNESS F22 isn't a compromise machine — it's a legitimate all-in-one that delivers 90% of what a full commercial gym rack setup does, for a fraction of the cost and space. The 4.6-star rating from 229 buyers reflects that. It's not for elite powerlifters or those who want a minimalist setup. But for the home lifter who wants to squat, press, row, and do cable work without sacrificing half their garage, this is arguably the best value rack at its price point.

**Buy it if** you want a complete home gym in one package. **Skip it if** you need competition-grade steel or only do basic barbell lifts. The assembly is a pain, but you'll forget the hours of setup the first time you use the smooth cable system.
