**Is the TEETER EP-560 the real deal for back pain relief, or just another overpriced stretching rack?**

Here's the thing about inversion tables: most of them are flimsy, awkward, and end up in the garage after three uses. The TEETER EP-560 is different—and the 4.5-star average across 225 Amazon ratings backs that up. But "different" doesn't mean "perfect." Let me break down exactly what you're getting for your money.

**What's actually in the box**

The EP-560 ships as a partial assembly. You get the steel A-frame, the bed (which TEETER calls the "table"), the patented ankle locking system, and a set of accessories that actually matter: a lumbar bridge for enhanced decompression, an AC adapter for the optional heat therapy, and foam grips for the handles. The assembly manual is clear, but budget 30–45 minutes and have a second person available. The frame is heavy-duty steel—rated to 300 pounds—and the bed is a breathable mesh that won't leave your back soaked after a session.

**Performance: does it actually relieve back pain?**

The EP-560's core differentiator is the ratchet-based ankle lock. Unlike the foam rollers on cheaper tables, this system clamps your ankles securely with a pull-handle release. Once you're locked in, you can invert gradually—anywhere from 0 to 90 degrees—using the hand levers. The pivot point is adjustable to match your height (the table fits users from 4'10" to 6'6"), which is crucial because a misaligned pivot makes inversion feel unstable.

What buyers consistently praise is the decompression effect. One verified reviewer noted, "I have L4-L5 herniation and after two weeks of 2-minute sessions, I can actually bend over without that sharp shooting pain." Another said, "The lumbar bridge is the game changer—it targets the lower spine way better than just hanging." That matches the clinical rationale: inversion creates negative pressure in the spinal discs, which can reduce nerve compression and improve fluid exchange.

**Where it falls short**

The EP-560 isn't a miracle device. The ratchet system requires you to be flexible enough to reach the release handles while inverted—if you're severely out of shape, you'll need a spotter initially. The heat therapy function is lukewarm at best; it's a gentle warmth, not deep tissue heat. And at this price point, the lack of a built-in tension gauge (like the higher-end EP-960) means you're estimating your decompression force.

Also, be honest with yourself about your health. Inversion increases blood pressure and heart rate. If you have uncontrolled hypertension, glaucoma, or are pregnant, this table is not for you—and TEETER's manual says so clearly.

**Who should buy this**

- **Desk workers with chronic lower back stiffness**—daily 2-minute sessions can counteract the effects of sitting.
- **Post-physical-therapy patients** looking to maintain spinal decompression at home, provided your PT approves.
- **People who've tried foam rollers and stretching** without relief—this is a more targeted approach.

Skip it if you're looking for a cardio workout or if you have mobility issues that prevent you from getting on/off the table safely.

**FAQ**

**Q: How long should I invert per session?**
A: Start with 1–2 minutes at a 20–30 degree angle. Gradually increase to 5 minutes over two weeks. More is not better—listen to your body.

**Q: Does the AC adapter really help?**
A: It provides mild heat to the lumbar area, which can relax muscles before decompression. It's a nice bonus, but don't expect a heating pad experience.

**Q: Is it easy to fold for storage?**
A: Yes—the frame folds flat in about 10 seconds and rolls on the built-in wheels. It takes up about 2 square feet of floor space when stored.

**The Verdict**

**Buy it if:** You have physician-approved back pain and want a durable, FDA-registered device that actually targets spinal decompression. The ratchet system and included accessories make it the best value in TEETER's lineup.

**Skip it if:** You're under 150 lbs and only have mild tension—a cheaper foam roller might suffice. Or if you have any cardiovascular issues—this isn't worth the risk.
