The FEIERDUN RK4 isn't just another squat stand with a pull-up bar bolted on. This is a full power cage that's trying to replace three separate machines in a home gym. The question is whether it does any of those jobs well enough to justify the footprint. After digging through 220 ratings and the specs, here's what actually matters.

## What You're Actually Getting

The box shows up heavy — around 350 pounds of steel split across multiple cartons. Inside you'll find the 2x2-inch 11-gauge steel uprights, a cable crossover system, landmine attachment, dip bars, J-hooks, safety pins, and a multi-grip pull-up bar. The base footprint is roughly 50 inches deep by 40 inches wide, so it fits in a standard garage or spare bedroom corner without dominating the space.

The standout feature is the integrated cable system. It's a 2:1 ratio, which means you're moving half the weight on the stack — this is the same setup used in most commercial lat pulldown machines. The 440-pound weight capacity means you're not going to outgrow this for a long time.

## Build Quality and Real-World Performance

Let's be direct: this cage is built like a tank in the places that matter. The 11-gauge steel is the same thickness used in racks costing twice as much. The Westside hole spacing — 1-inch increments through the bench zone — is a feature you'd expect on a $1,000+ rack, and it makes adjusting the J-hooks for bench press versus squat genuinely quick.

During heavy squats (300+ pounds), the rack stays planted. The 12 floor-mounting points help, but even without bolting it down, the weight distribution keeps things stable. The safety pins are thick and slide smoothly into the holes without binding.

The cable system is where opinions split. At first, it runs smooth. But multiple reviewers note the pulleys develop a squeak within a few months. One buyer put it plainly: "The cables work fine but you will be spraying silicone lubricant on the pulleys every couple of months." That's maintenance you don't deal with on a dedicated cable machine, but for the price, it's a tradeoff.

## What Buyers Consistently Praise

The most common thread in positive reviews is the value-to-function ratio. One verified purchaser wrote: "This replaced my separate squat rack, lat pulldown machine, and dip station. Saved me floor space and about $800." Another noted the versatility: "I can do full-body workouts without leaving the rack. Pull-ups, rows, presses, squats, and curls — everything's here."

The multi-grip pull-up bar gets consistent mention, with the angled grips being a favorite for neutral-grip pull-ups that are easier on shoulders.

## Where It Falls Short

The J-hooks are the weakest link. They're bare steel, which means they'll scratch your barbell's knurling. It's not a functional issue, but if you care about your bar's finish, plan on wrapping the hooks with athletic tape or buying aftermarket lined J-hooks.

Assembly is a genuine pain point. Plan for 3-4 hours with two people. The instructions are the typical translated-from-Chinese style with unclear diagrams. Several reviewers mentioned having to backtrack because bolt sizes weren't clearly labeled.

The plastic pulleys are the other compromise. They work, but they're the first thing that'll need attention. Budget for lubricant or plan to upgrade to metal pulleys down the road.

## Who Should Buy This

This rack is ideal for the intermediate lifter who wants a complete home gym without spending $2,000+. If you're currently juggling a cheap squat stand and a separate cable attachment, this consolidates everything into one footprint.

Skip it if you're a competitive powerlifter who needs a 3x3-inch rack with 1,000-pound capacity, or if you don't have patience for a long assembly session. Also skip if you only do basic squats and presses — you'd be paying for features you won't use.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I deadlift inside this rack?**
A: Yes, the base is wide enough for a standard 4x6-foot platform, and the uprights are far enough apart that the bar won't hit them. Just keep the safeties set low.

**Q: Does the cable system include weight plates?**
A: No, this is a plate-loaded system. You'll need your own Olympic plates (2-inch hole). The cable attachments use a standard clip that fits most carabiners.

**Q: What's the ceiling height requirement?**
A: The rack is 83.5 inches tall. You'll want at least 90 inches of clearance for pull-ups and to avoid scraping the ceiling with the bar.

## The Verdict

**Buy it if** you want a multi-station home gym in one footprint and don't mind occasional maintenance on the pulleys. **Skip it if** you're a minimalist lifter who just needs a solid squat rack — you can get a simpler cage for less.

The FEIERDUN RK4 earns a 4.2/5. It's not perfect — the pulleys and J-hooks need attention — but for the price, it replaces three machines and holds up where it counts. Just clear your Saturday for assembly.
