**This isn't a squat rack. It's a leverage gym that happens to include a squat rack.**

That distinction matters, because the DONOW Leverage Gym Power Rack solves the biggest problem home lifters face: training heavy alone. The lever arms use a pivot-based system where the weight sits behind you, not on your shoulders. When you fail a rep, you just push forward and the safeties catch it. No crash bars. No panicked roll of shame. Just set it down and reset.

At $600-700 (price fluctuates), you're getting a 2x2-inch 11-gauge steel frame with a 1,000-pound weight capacity on the rack itself. That's respectable for the price point. The lever arms handle 400 lbs per side, which covers 95% of home lifters.

## What's Actually in the Box

The package is heavy — expect around 250 lbs of steel spread across multiple boxes. You get:

- The main power rack frame with J-hooks and pin safeties
- Two lever arms with rotating handles and weight posts
- A LAT pulldown station with a 200-lb max weight stack capability (plates not included)
- A low row footplate attachment
- Pull-up bar integrated into the top crossmember
- All hardware, but **bring your own tools** — the included Allen wrenches strip easily

As shown in the product image, the rack stands 84 inches tall with a 40x48-inch footprint. If your ceiling is under 8 feet, measure twice before ordering — this won't fit in most basements.

## Real-World Performance

The lever squat is the headline act here, and it delivers. Because the weight path is fixed by the pivot point, your form self-corrects. You're forced into a vertical bar path, which translates directly to barbell back squat strength. One buyer put it bluntly: *"I've added 40 lbs to my barbell squat in 3 months just using the lever arms on off days. The fixed path is a feature, not a bug."*

The LAT pulldown is serviceable but not spectacular. The cable runs smoothly through the pulley system, and the seat padding is dense enough for 45-minute sessions. But the 200-lb capacity will frustrate anyone who's been training seriously for more than a year. You can't add more weight without upgrading the entire cable system.

## What Buyers Consistently Praise

The safety aspect dominates positive reviews. Over 229 ratings at 4.6 stars, and the recurring theme is confidence under heavy loads. One verified purchase wrote: *"Failed a 315-lb lever squat at rep 5. The safeties caught it instantly. No noise, no drama, just racked it and took a breather."*

The multi-function design also earns consistent praise. For apartment dwellers with limited space, this replaces a squat rack, a leg press, a lat pulldown machine, and a low row station. That's four pieces of commercial equipment in one corner of a bedroom.

## Where It Falls Short

Assembly is the biggest hurdle. Expect 3-4 hours with two people, and even then, some bolt holes require persuasion. A few buyers reported needing a rubber mallet and metal file to get everything aligned. The manual is a single sheet of exploded diagrams — no step-by-step guidance.

The J-hooks are functional but basic. They lack the UHMW plastic lining of higher-end racks, so you'll hear metal-on-metal scraping when racking a barbell. A $15 roll of UHMW tape fixes this permanently.

Finally, the weight storage pegs sit on the rear uprights at fixed heights. If you're storing 45s, they'll be at shin height — awkward to load and unload repeatedly. Not a dealbreaker, but annoying.

## Who Should Buy This

**Buy it if:** You train alone and want to push heavy squats without a spotter. You have a 7x7-foot corner to dedicate to a home gym. You want multiple exercises from one machine and don't mind a 4-hour assembly session.

**Skip it if:** You're over 6'2" — the lever arms have a limited range that tall lifters will hit early. You're a powerlifter who needs more than 200 lbs on cable movements. You want something that assembles in under an hour.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I use this for bench press?**
A: Yes, but it's awkward. The lever arms pivot from the bottom, so bench pressing requires setting the pivot height low. It works, but a dedicated bench station would be better. The J-hooks accommodate a barbell for traditional bench if you have a separate bench.

**Q: What's the actual ceiling height needed?**
A: The rack is 84 inches tall. Add 6-8 inches for the pull-up bar clearance and your head during squats. Minimum 90-inch ceiling, ideally 96 inches.

**Q: Does the LAT pulldown require a weight stack?**
A: No, it uses standard Olympic plates loaded onto a post. You'll need your own plates — nothing included except the frame and pulleys.

## The Verdict

**4.6/5 — Buy it if you want a compact, spotter-free heavy training station. Skip it if you're tall or need serious cable weight.**

The DONOW Leverage Gym Power Rack is the best budget solution I've reviewed for solo lifters who want to squat heavy without fear. The lever system is genuinely clever, the frame is solid, and the multi-function design justifies the floor space. Assembly is a pain and the LAT station has limits, but for the price, nothing else packs this much capability into one footprint.

Just budget a weekend for setup and buy a tube of UHMW tape before your first session.
